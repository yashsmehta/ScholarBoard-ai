# Alice J. O'Toole

*computational face and person recognition* — The University of Texas at Dallas, School of Behavioral and Brain Sciences, Face Perception Research Lab.

## Background

Alice J. O'Toole is a Professor and holder of the Aage and Margareta Møller Endowed Chair, conducting research that integrates psychology, computer science, and neuroscience to examine human and machine perception of faces and bodies. O'Toole's work utilizes deep convolutional neural networks (DCNNs) and machine learning to model high-level visual representations, comparing algorithmic performance with human expertise in forensic face identification. They use methodologies such as psychophysics and functional neuroimaging to investigate how identity, gender, and viewpoint are encoded within neural populations and computational architectures. Recent investigations by O'Toole focus on the robustness of face representations in real-world viewing conditions and the potential biases inherent in automated recognition systems.

## Papers

### 2025 — Unlocking the power of partnership: How humans and machines can work together to improve face recognition
*arXiv preprint*
Authors: P. Jonathon Phillips, Géraldine Jeckeln, Carina A. Hahn, Amy N. Yates, P. C. Fontana, Alice J. O'Toole

This study examines the computational and psychological conditions under which human-machine collaboration optimizes face identification accuracy. The researchers propose the Proximal Accuracy Rule (PAR), which suggests that the efficacy of statistical fusion between two agents—human or machine—is inversely related to the difference in their baseline performance levels. By analyzing data from forensic experts and untrained individuals alongside state-of-the-art algorithms, the authors identify a substantial 'critical fusion zone' where combining a human reviewer with a superior algorithm still enhances overall system performance. The work provides an evidence-based framework for intelligent human-AI integration, utilizing graph matching theory to optimize partnerships and minimize the impact of individual human errors on high-stakes identification tasks.

### 2025 — Constructing a cross-race face identity triad test
*Behavior Research Methods*
Authors: Géraldine Jeckeln, Alice J. O'Toole

The authors introduce the Cross-Race Face Identity Triad (CR-FIT) test, a rigorous diagnostic instrument designed to measure the other-race effect (ORE) while eliminating the confound of response bias. Unlike traditional matching tasks, the CR-FIT employs image triads—consisting of two images of a target identity and one foil—forcing a 3-alternative choice that standardizes the decision criterion across observers. To ensure that performance disparities reflect perceptual expertise rather than stimulus variance, the researchers utilized deep convolutional neural network (DCNN) similarity scores and human pilot data to match the difficulty of African American and Caucasian face sets. Findings confirm a robust ORE across diverse populations, establishing the test as a suitable metric for assessing face matching proficiency in both forensic and research contexts.

### 2025 — The Early Bird Identifies the Worm: You Can't Beat a Head Start in Long-Term Body Re-ID (ECHO-BID)
*arXiv preprint*
Authors: Thomas M. Metz, Matthew Q. Hill, Alice J. O'Toole

This research investigates the transferability of vision foundation models (VFMs) to the problem of long-term person re-identification (re-id) in unconstrained environments characterized by viewpoint variance, clothing changes, and distance. The authors evaluate four prominent VFMs (CLIP, DINOv2, AIMv2, and EVA-02) and demonstrate that domain-specific fine-tuning of these large-scale backbones allows them to significantly outperform traditional specialized re-id models. The proposed ECHO-BID (Eva Clothes-Change from Hidden Objects - Body IDentification) model, based on the EVA-02 Large architecture, demonstrates superior robustness to occlusion and environmental covariates. Ablation studies reveal that the scale of pretraining and the complexity of the transfer learning protocol are the primary drivers of this performance advantage in identifying people across temporal gaps.

### 2025 — Dissecting human body representations in deep networks trained for person identification
*arXiv preprint / 19th IEEE International Conference on Face and Gesture Recognition · 1 citations*
Authors: Thomas M. Metz, Matthew Q. Hill, Blake Myers, Veda Nandan Gandi, Rahul Chilakapati, Alice J. O'Toole

This paper provides a granular analysis of the latent representational structures within deep neural networks optimized for long-term body identification. By probing the embeddings of four distinct architectures (including Vision Transformers and CNNs) trained on millions of person images, the authors show that these models implicitly extract facial identity features and encode social categories like gender despite lacking explicit supervision for these traits. Furthermore, image-based covariates such as viewpoint (yaw) and source dataset origin are found to be linearly decodable from the embeddings. Critically, the study demonstrates that identity verification accuracy can be enhanced by performing comparisons within specific principal component subspaces, effectively filtering out noise-related variance without necessitating model retraining.

### 2024 — Deep convolutional neural networks are sensitive to face configuration
*Journal of Vision · 1 citations*
Authors: Virginia E. Strehle, Natalie K. Bendiksen, Alice J. O'Toole

The study investigates whether deep convolutional neural networks (DCNNs) trained for face identification utilize configural information (the spatial arrangement of features) similar to the human visual system. Using a diagnostic paradigm that independently manipulates facial features (e.g., swapping eyes) and facial configuration (e.g., altering inter-ocular distance), the researchers quantified the resulting representational shifts in DCNN embedding space. While both feature and configural changes significantly altered the networks' outputs, sensitivity to configural information was found to increase significantly across hierarchical layers, far exceeding the sensitivity of low-level pixel representations. These findings suggest that DCNNs emerge as robust models of human-like configural processing, reconciliating computational efficiency with biological principles of face expertise.
