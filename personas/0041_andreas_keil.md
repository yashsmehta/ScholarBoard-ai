# Andreas Keil

*visual attention and emotional perception* — University of Florida, Department of Psychology, Laboratory for Brain, Body, and Behavior.

## Background

Andreas Keil is a Professor of Psychology at the University of Florida, where they direct the Laboratory for Brain, Body, and Behavior. Keil’s research examines the neurobiological mechanisms through which human sensation and perception adapt to motivational and emotional states to facilitate survival and well-being. Using dense-array electroencephalography (EEG), steady-state visual evoked potentials (ssVEPs), and simultaneous EEG-fMRI, Keil investigates how aversive learning and threat expectancy modulate neural gain in the visual cortex. They also focus on developing and validating signal processing methods for characterizing oscillatory brain dynamics and establishing international guidelines for electrophysiological research.

## Papers

### 2025 — Comparing Methods for Mass Univariate Analyses of Human EEG: Empirical Data and Simulations
*bioRxiv*
Authors: Anna-Lena Tebbe, Christian Panitz, Andreas Keil

This methodological investigation assesses the performance and validity of various mass univariate statistical frameworks applied to scalp-recorded human electroencephalography (EEG) data. Utilizing both empirical datasets and Monte Carlo simulations, the researchers evaluate the sensitivity, specificity, and family-wise error rate control of common techniques, including Cluster-Based Permutation Testing (CBPT), Threshold-Free Cluster Enhancement (TFCE), and False Discovery Rate (FDR) corrections. The study characterizes how these methods manage varying signal-to-noise ratios, temporal autocorrelations, and spatial dependencies within neural time series. Results provide evidence-based recommendations for standardizing EEG analysis pipelines to optimize the detection of true neurophysiological effects while minimizing Type I errors in high-density sensor arrays.

### 2025 — Emotional Responses to Naturalistic and AI-generated Affective Pictures: A Systematic Comparison
*bioRxiv*
Authors: Faith Gilbert, Fan Yang, Ethan Smith, Mingzhou Ding, Ruogu Fang, Andreas Keil

This study systematically compares cortical and autonomic reactions to affectively charged images sourced from established naturalistic databases versus those synthesized using generative artificial intelligence. By recording steady-state visual evoked potentials (ssVEPs), skin conductance responses, and subjective ratings of valence and arousal, the authors examine whether AI-generated stimuli reliably recruit the same motivational systems—defense and appetitive—as traditional photographic stimuli. The findings quantify the extent to which synthetic imagery facilitates early visuocortical gain and late evaluative processing, addressing the validity of utilizing generative AI in affective neuroscience research and personalized psychological paradigms.

### 2025 — Representational Competition of Spatially and Temporally Overlapped Target and Distractor
*bioRxiv*
Authors: Katherine McCain, Wendel Matthew Friedl, Gilles Pourtois, Faisal Mushtaq, Yuri Pavlov, Andreas Keil

The research explores the neurophysiological mechanisms of sensory competition within the human visual system when targets and distractors exhibit high spatial and temporal overlap. Utilizing a frequency-tagging paradigm (ssVEPs) to isolate the neural processing of concurrent stimuli, the study identifies a biphasic temporal profile of resource allocation. Specifically, results indicate that an initial period of feedforward facilitation is followed by significant competitive suppression of distractor representations in early visual cortex. Comparisons with formal models of divisive normalization suggest that the observed competitive interactions are driven by an interplay of bottom-up stimulus salience and top-down attentional prioritization, providing insights into how the brain adjudicates between competing sensory inputs in complex environments.

### 2025 — Concept2Brain: An AI model for predicting subject-level neurophysiological responses to text and pictures
*bioRxiv · 1 citations*
Authors: Alejandro Santos-Mayo, Faith Gilbert, Arash Mirifar, Anna-Lena Tebbe, Ruogu Fang, Mingzhou Ding, Andreas Keil

This work introduces Concept2Brain, a deep generative architecture designed to predict individual-level EEG responses to complex semantic content across visual and linguistic modalities. The model integrates Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training (CLIP) embeddings to represent conceptual space and a conditional Variational Autoencoder (cVAE) to map these representations onto the latent space of neurophysiological dynamics. By training on a large-scale corpus of affective responses, the model successfully reconstructs canonical event-related potential (ERP) components, such as the N170 and the Late Positive Potential (LPP), without explicit supervision. The model demonstrates significant generalization ability across novel stimuli and participants, establishing a predictive framework for simulating human brain activity in response to varied conceptual information.

### 2025 — Neurophysiological and Autonomic Dynamics of Threat Processing during Sustained Social Fear Generalization
*Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience · 4 citations*
Authors: Jourdan J. Pouliot, Richard T. Ward, Caitlin M. Traiser, Payton Chiasson, Faith E. Gilbert, Andreas Keil

This study investigates the longitudinal dynamics of aversive generalization using facial identities across several hundred trials to simulate prolonged social engagement. By recording steady-state visual evoked potentials (ssVEPs), alpha-band power, and pupillary dilation, the researchers tracked the evolution of neural tuning functions from initial conditioning to sustained learning. The results show that while early learning is marked by sharpened visuocortical discrimination—where sensory resources are selectively deployed to the conditioned threat (CS+)—prolonged exposure leads to a divergence: autonomic measures exhibit broad generalization, whereas sensory cortical representations maintain or increase their sharpening. These findings suggest that the human brain implements distinct temporal dynamics for sensory-level discrimination versus higher-order motivational generalization in social fear contexts.
