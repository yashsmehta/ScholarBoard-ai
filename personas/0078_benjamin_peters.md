# Benjamin Peters

*computational vision and NeuroAI* — University of Edinburgh, School of Informatics, Generative AI Laboratory (GAIL).

## Background

Benjamin Peters is a Lecturer in Computational Cognitive Science at the University of Edinburgh and a fellow of the Generative AI Laboratory. Their research integrates cognitive science, neuroscience, and artificial intelligence to investigate how humans and machines perceive structure and relations within complex, dynamic environments. Peters specializes in developing brain-computational neural network models that serve as mechanistic explanations for human behavior, eye movements, and neural data. Awarded a UKRI Future Leaders Fellowship, Peters previously held research positions at the University of Glasgow and Columbia University focusing on the intersection of generative and discriminative computations in vision.

## Papers

### 2025 — Using Transfer Learning to Identify a Neural System's Algorithm
*Proceedings of the Annual Meeting of the Cognitive Science Society (CogSci)*
Authors: John Morrison, Nikolaus Kriegeskorte, Benjamin Peters

Standard behavior-based and parts-based analytical methods often fail to differentiate between distinct algorithms that produce equivalent input-output mappings. This study proposes identifying a neural system's algorithm by its transfer learning profile, which quantifies the relative efficiency with which it learns novel mappings. Using artificial neural networks (ANNs), we performed six experiments across three task domains: a linear algebraic task, 2D image classification, and 3D nonlinear image classification. We demonstrate that the transfer learning profile effectively recovers the system's original algorithm even when standard behavioral metrics and activation/weight-based analyses fail to reveal these mechanistic differences. We conclude that this framework provides a robust foundation for attributing specific algorithms to neural systems in cognitive science and machine learning.

### 2025 — Neural correlates of working memory precision
*Journal of Vision*
Authors: Philipp Deutsch, Cora Fischer, Jochen Kaiser, Christoph Bledowski, Benjamin Peters

The neuronal basis of capacity limitations in working memory (WM) remains a central question in cognitive neuroscience. Unlike standard paradigms that probe a single item, we employed a whole-report task requiring participants to memorize one, two, or four orientations of Gabor gratings and sequentially report them with accompanying confidence ratings. We recorded neural activity using magnetoencephalography (MEG) and utilized beamforming for source-level estimation. Applying multivariate pattern analysis (MVPA) across the cortical surface, we found that activity patterns in posterior cortical regions significantly predicted the aggregated precision of orientation reports (a composite metric of confidence and error). This predictive capability was strongest during the encoding phase and persisted until report. These results suggest that WM capacity is fundamentally limited by the precision of initial stimulus processing in early sensory and posterior regions, supporting the sensory recruitment hypothesis.
