# David Watson

*social and dynamic face perception* — University of York, Department of Psychology.

## Background

David M. Watson is a vision neuroscientist at the University of York specializing in the functional architecture of high-level visual systems. Their research integrates functional MRI, transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), and behavioral psychophysics to delineate the neural substrates of scene perception and social face processing. Watson examines how the human brain represents dynamic facial behaviors and the organizational principles of object and scene recognition. Their current work investigates the connectivity and functional specialization of the lateral and ventral visual pathways in processing complex social information.

## Papers

### 2025 — Functional Connectivity of the Scene Processing Network at Rest Does Not Reliably Predict Human Behavior on Scene Processing Tasks
*eNeuro*
Authors: David M. Watson, Timothy J. Andrews

This study evaluated whether interindividual variations in resting-state functional connectivity (RSFC) within the human scene-selective network correlate with cognitive performance in scene recognition, spatial memory, and navigation. Utilizing a large-scale public dataset (N=217), the researchers measured functional connectivity among core regions including the occipital place area (OPA), parahippocampal place area (PPA), and retrosplenial complex (RSC). While the analysis successfully replicated the known posterior–anterior connectivity bias within the network, the results failed to show any consistent associations between intrinsic network connectivity and behavioral task scores. These findings suggest that resting-state dynamics may not sufficiently reflect the neural substrates of scene perception and navigation, highlighting the potential necessity for task-based or naturalistic fMRI designs to uncover the neural basis of these behavioral traits.

### 2025 — A data-driven analysis of the perceptual and neural responses to natural objects reveals organising principles of human visual cognition
*Journal of Neuroscience*
Authors: David M. Watson, Timothy J. Andrews

The authors employed a data-driven approach to investigate the functional organization of the human visual cortex using 720 object concepts from the THINGS initiative. By applying Partial Least Squares Regression (PLSR) to link 66 perceptual stimulus dimensions with whole-brain fMRI responses, they identified five latent components that accurately predicted neural activity across both low- and high-level visual areas. These components revealed smooth, graded topographic representations and captured essential object properties such as animacy, real-world size, and category. Because these identified dimensions did not align simply with traditional categorical models, the study suggests that the visual brain's organization is primarily driven by the statistical properties of objects encountered in the natural world.

### 2024 — View-symmetric representations of faces in human and artificial neural networks
*Neuropsychologia*
Authors: Xun Zhu, David M. Watson, Daniel Rogers, Timothy J. Andrews

This investigation explored how the human visual system and deep convolutional neural networks (DCNNs) represent facial identity across varying viewpoints. The study specifically focused on the emergence of 'view-symmetric' representations, in which mirror-symmetric views (e.g., -45° and +45°) are processed as more similar to each other than to other views. Using fMRI and computational modeling, the researchers demonstrated that view-symmetry is a prominent organizational feature in the later stages of the ventral visual stream and in the deeper layers of identity-trained DCNNs. These results indicate that view-symmetry serves as an important intermediate computational step in achieving viewpoint-invariant face recognition.

### 2024 — Mapping the functional and structural connectivity of the scene network
*Human Brain Mapping*
Authors: David M. Watson, Timothy J. Andrews

Combining resting-state fMRI, naturalistic viewing fMRI (using 'Game of Thrones' stimuli), and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), this research characterized the extrinsic connectivity of the core scene-selective network. The analysis revealed a robust posterior–anterior connectivity bias: posterior scene regions (OPA and posterior PPA) showed preferential functional and structural links to early visual and posterior parietal regions, whereas anterior regions (RSC and anterior PPA) exhibited stronger connectivity with mnemonic and default mode network areas. The findings support a hierarchical organization within the scene network, where posterior regions are primarily involved in representing egocentric visual features and anterior regions support higher-level memory-based scene processing.

### 2023 — Connectopic mapping techniques do not reflect functional gradients in the brain
*NeuroImage*
Authors: David M. Watson, Timothy J. Andrews

This methodological study critically examines 'connectopic mapping,' a technique used to identify functional gradients (e.g., retinotopy) from fMRI connectivity data. The researchers generated synthetic datasets of Gaussian white noise and processed them using standard fMRI pipelines, including spatial smoothing and interpolation across coordinate spaces. They found that these common preprocessing steps introduce spatial autocorrelations that generate illusory connectopic gradients, which closely resemble real biological maps such as eccentricity and polar angle. The study warns that many gradients reported in the connectivity literature may be artifacts of data processing rather than true reflections of the brain's functional architecture.
