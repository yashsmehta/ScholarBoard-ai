# Diane M. Beck

*visual attention and perception* — University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, Department of Psychology, Beck Lab.

## Background

Beck is a Professor and Head of the Department of Psychology at the University of Illinois, Urbana-Champaign, where they investigate the neural and cognitive constraints of visual representation. Their research characterizes how top-down mechanisms like attention and real-world statistical regularities modulate activity in the visual cortex to facilitate scene category recognition and object awareness. Employing functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), electroencephalography (EEG), and transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), Beck explores the limits of human visual processing and the role of neural oscillations in conscious perception. Recent investigations in their lab also utilize deep neural networks to model the features that define representative exemplars of scene categories.

## Papers

### 2025 — What makes good exemplars of a scene category good? Evidence from deep neural nets
*Vision Research*
Authors: Pei-Ling Yang, Diane M. Beck

This study employs behavioral psychophysics, functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), and deep convolutional neural networks (DCNNs) to explore the neural and computational mechanisms underlying the processing advantage for representative scene exemplars. The authors demonstrate that 'good' exemplars—scenes that are highly representative of their category—are detected and categorized with higher sensitivity and faster reaction times than 'bad' exemplars. Multivariate pattern analysis (MVPA) of fMRI data revealed that these statistically regular stimuli evoke more distinct category representations in the parahippocampal place area (PPA), retrosplenial cortex (RSC), and primary visual cortex (V1). The findings suggest that the visual system utilizes learned environmental regularities as internal predictions, facilitating the categorization process by minimizing prediction error for typical inputs.

### 2024 — Real-world Statistical Regularity Impacts Inattentional Blindness
*Consciousness and Cognition*
Authors: Ling Lee Chong, Diane M. Beck

This research investigates whether the brain's sensitivity to real-world statistical regularities—patterns of typicality and probability learned over a lifetime—influences the likelihood of stimuli reaching conscious awareness. Utilizing a sustained inattentional blindness paradigm across two large-scale experiments, the authors compared noticing rates for unexpected stimuli that were either probable (statistically regular) or improbable (statistically irregular) within their environmental context. The results indicate significantly higher rates of inattentional blindness for improbable scenes. This suggests that the visual system's predictive mechanisms prioritize stimuli that conform to internal models of the world, thereby modulating the probability that an item will successfully capture attention and enter conscious perception.

### 2024 — Highly Memorable Images Are More Readily Perceived
*Journal of Experimental Psychology: General · 10 citations*
Authors: Will Deng, Kara D. Federmeier, Diane M. Beck

This investigation examines the relationship between image memorability, an intrinsic property of visual stimuli, and the efficiency of early perceptual processing. Using an intact/scrambled discrimination task and an adaptive staircase procedure, the authors quantified the minimum presentation duration required for reliable detection of scenes varying in memorability. Across multiple experiments, highly memorable images were found to have significantly lower detection thresholds than low-memorability images. The researchers argue that memorable stimuli are processed more efficiently because they more closely align with stored, memory-based perceptual templates, thereby facilitating more rapid template matching during the initial stages of visual recognition.

### 2024 — The Brain's Sensitivity to Real-world Statistical Regularity Does Not Require Full Attention
*Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience · 11 citations*
Authors: Evan G. Center, Kara D. Federmeier, Diane M. Beck

This study assesses whether the neural mechanisms that utilize real-world statistical regularities to facilitate visual processing require focused attention. Using event-related potentials (ERPs), specifically the N300 component associated with high-level perceptual template matching, the authors recorded brain activity during a dual-task paradigm. Participants were presented with scene-object pairs that varied in category representativeness while simultaneously performing a demanding secondary task to manipulate attentional load. The N300 amplitude was systematically modulated by representativeness (exemplar goodness) regardless of the level of attentional load. These results provide evidence that the visual system's predictive modeling based on environmental regularities is a robust, pre-attentive process that can occur even when cognitive resources are diverted.

### 2024 — Bypassing input to V1 in visual awareness: A TMS-EROS investigation
*Neuropsychologia · 3 citations*
Authors: Ramisha S. Knight, Tao Chen, Evan G. Center, Gabriele Gratton, Monica Fabiani, Silvia Savazzi, Chiara Mazzi, Diane M. Beck

This research utilizes a multimodal approach combining transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) and Event-Related Optical Signal (EROS) imaging to determine if recurrent feedback to the primary visual cortex (V1) is a prerequisite for conscious visual experience. By applying single-pulse TMS over the posterior parietal cortex (PPC) to induce phosphenes, the authors bypassed the traditional retino-geniculo-striate pathway. EROS data showed that awareness of induced phosphenes was associated with activity in the PPC at approximately 50 ms and subsequent activation in the cuneus and early visual cortex (V1/V2) at 140 ms. The presence of awareness-related signatures in both dorsal and early ventral regions, even when the initial V1 input sweep was bypassed, supports interactive models of consciousness where recurrent loops of activity are necessary for the emergence of awareness.
