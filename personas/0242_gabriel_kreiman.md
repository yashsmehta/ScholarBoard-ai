# Gabriel Kreiman

*Computational neuroscience and AI* — Harvard University, Department of Ophthalmology, Kreiman Lab.

## Background

Gabriel Kreiman is a Professor of Ophthalmology at Harvard Medical School and a researcher at the F.M. Kirby Neurobiology Center at Boston Children's Hospital. Kreiman’s research integrates high-resolution intracranial neurophysiology in the human brain with computational modeling and psychophysics to study how neural circuits encode, process, and transmit information. This work addresses the neuronal correlates of visual object recognition, memory formation, and the development of biologically-inspired artificial intelligence models. Their current projects focus on elucidating the mechanisms of visual cognition—such as pattern completion and context-dependent recognition—while building AI architectures that mimic human-like visual perception and learning.

## Papers

### 2025 — Single-neuron encoding of rapidly learned visual information reshapes human perception
*bioRxiv*
Authors: Marcelo Armendariz, Julie Blumberg, Jed Singer, Franz Aiple, Jiye Kim, Nuria Dominguez-Iturza, Armin Brandt, Peter Reinacher, Andreas Schulze-Bonhage, Gabriel Kreiman

The study investigates the neurophysiological basis of rapid visual object recognition through single-neuron recordings in the human occipital (OC) cortex and medial temporal lobe (MTL). Utilizing a paradigm involving the recognition of degraded images, the authors demonstrate that neurons in both regions modulate their firing patterns to encode newly acquired visual information and drive perceptual shifts. Population decoding reveals that OC neurons exhibit increased processing latencies during the identification of learned images, which consequently delays response onsets in the MTL. These findings suggest that OC-mediated recognition following rapid learning involves extensive recurrent processing—potentially via higher-order neocortical feedback—independent of direct MTL feedback, providing mechanistic constraints for biologically plausible few-shot learning models.

### 2025 — HumorDB: Can AI understand graphical humor?
*International Conference on Computer Vision (ICCV)*
Authors: Vedaant Jain, Felipe dos Santos Alves Feitosa, Gabriel Kreiman

This work introduces HumorDB, a curated dataset designed to evaluate the capacity of AI systems to interpret graphical humor, serving as a benchmark for complex scene understanding and high-level cognitive integration. The dataset includes diverse visual formats—including photos, cartoons, and sketches—and incorporates minimally contrastive image pairs to isolate humor-inducing features. Evaluation of state-of-the-art vision models and large vision-language models (VLMs) across classification, rating, and comparison tasks reveals a substantial performance gap compared to human benchmarks. Saliency and attention map analyses indicate that models often fail to localize the specific incongruities that elicit humor, suggesting that human-like humor understanding requires more robust architectures capable of contextual reasoning and bridging the gap between perception and abstraction.

### 2025 — Neural correlates of minimal recognizable configurations in the human brain
*Cell Reports*
Authors: Antonino Casile, Aurelie Cordier, Jiye G. Kim, Andrea Cometa, Joseph R. Madsen, Scellig Stone, Guy Ben-Yosef, Shimon Ullman, William Anderson, Gabriel Kreiman

This research explores the neural substrates of pattern completion using minimally recognizable configurations (MIRCs) and their unrecognizable sub-parts (sub-MIRCs). Through invasive human neurophysiological recordings (iEEG), the authors show that successful MIRC identification is characterized by a dynamic interplay of feedforward and feedback signals between frontal and temporal cortices. Furthermore, the study finds that sub-MIRC recognition improves significantly following exposure to the original full objects, a process of rapid unsupervised learning reflected in modulated neural activity within the temporal cortex. These results are at odds with strictly feedforward architectures of visual recognition, highlighting the essential role of top-down signals from the frontal lobe in resolving visually ambiguous stimuli.

### 2025 — Tuned compositional feature replays for efficient stream learning
*IEEE Transactions on Neural Networks and Learning Systems (TNNLS)*
Authors: Morgan B. Talbot, Rushikesh Zawar, Rohil Badkundri, Mengmi Zhang, Gabriel Kreiman

Addressing the challenge of catastrophic forgetting in online stream learning, the authors propose Compositional Replay Using Memory Blocks (CRUMB), a continual learning algorithm. CRUMB mitigates knowledge loss by replaying feature maps reconstructed from a codebook of trainable and re-usable 'memory blocks.' In convolutional neural networks (CNNs), these vectors are concatenated to compositionally approximate feature map tensors, allowing the model to store only memory block indices rather than raw images, reducing the memory footprint to 3.6%. The reconstruction mechanism biases the network toward shape-based features over texture, enhancing stability during sequential task learning. Empirical evaluation across seven benchmarks demonstrates that CRUMB significantly outperforms thirteen existing methods in both mitigating forgetting and computational efficiency.

### 2025 — Trial-by-trial inter-areal interactions in visual cortex in the presence or absence of visual stimulation
*eLife*
Authors: Dianna Hidalgo, Giorgia Dellaferrera, Will Xiao, Maria Papadopouli, Stelios Smirnakis, Gabriel Kreiman

This study investigates single-trial inter-areal communication within the visual cortex of mice and macaques. Using calcium imaging and extracellular neurophysiological recordings, the authors quantify the predictability of neuronal activity in one cortical layer or area based on the activity of another, distinguishing between stimulus-driven and non-stimulus-driven shared variability. The results demonstrate bidirectional interactions between L2/3 and L4 in mice and V1 and V4 in macaques, with the latter showing directional asymmetry. The research identifies a bimodal distribution of neurons: those primarily driven by visual input and those whose activity remains highly predictable during spontaneous states. These findings highlight the significance of non-visual shared variability and provide a framework for computational models that account for the transformations from sensory inputs to cortical encoding.
