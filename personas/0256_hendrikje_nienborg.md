# Hendrikje Nienborg

*Visual decision making and perception* — NIH, Laboratory of Sensorimotor Research, Visual Decision Making Section.

## Background

Nienborg is a Senior Investigator at the National Eye Institute and the head of the Visual Decision Making Section. Their research investigates the neural mechanisms that integrate sensory signals with cognitive factors such as attention, motivation, and prior knowledge to guide behavior. Using large-scale electrophysiological recordings, optogenetics, and computational modeling, their lab explores how cortical circuits process information from central and peripheral visual fields. Nienborg’s work examines how decision-related feedback is broadcast across the visual cortex and the influence of spontaneous movements on sensory encoding in primates.

## Papers

### 2025 — Is the impact of spontaneous movements on early visual cortex species specific?
*Trends in Neurosciences*
Authors: Incheol Kang, Bharath Chandra Talluri, Jacob L. Yates, Cristopher M. Niell, Hendrikje Nienborg

This review evaluates the evidence regarding species-specific differences in movement-related neural modulation within the primary visual cortex (V1). While rodent studies consistently report large, brain-wide gain changes and substantial variance in V1 activity driven by locomotion and orofacial behaviors, recent investigations in macaques and marmosets show minimal direct modulation. The authors discuss how these discrepancies may arise from differences in behavioral state, the impact of eye movements on retinal input, eccentricity-dependent effects, and slow neural drift. They propose a functional taxonomy for movement signals to facilitate standardized cross-species comparisons in active vision research.

### 2024 — Decisions: Tracking the evolution of a single choice
*eLife*
Authors: Bharath Chandra Talluri, Hendrikje Nienborg

The authors discuss recent advancements in large-scale neural recording technologies that permit the direct observation of decision-related computations within single trials, moving beyond traditional trial-averaged analyses. Focusing on population activity in the macaque lateral intraparietal (LIP) area, the article highlights evidence that decision variables evolve toward a choice boundary in a manner consistent with drift-diffusion dynamics. This real-time resolution provides empirical support for the theory that decision-making involves the noisy integration of evidence over time, a mechanism previously difficult to resolve due to the high variability of individual neural responses.

### 2024 — Serotonergic neurons in the dorsal raphe regulate visual attention
*bioRxiv*
Authors: Aashay Patel, Katsuhisa Kawaguchi, Lenka Seillier, Hendrikje Nienborg

This study identifies the dorsal raphe nucleus (DRN) as a critical controller of visual attention via serotonergic signaling. Utilizing a behavioral model to track attention allocation in mice and simultaneous DRN fiber photometry, the researchers observed that serotonergic activity is suppressed during periods of focused attention on cued stimuli. Optogenetic activation of DRN-5HT neurons was sufficient to impair detection performance, while their inhibition enhanced it. Furthermore, serotonin levels in the visual cortex were found to decrease during active detection, establishing serotonin as a key neuromodulatory carrier of attentional state in the mammalian visual system.

### 2024 — Towards a simplified model of primary visual cortex
*bioRxiv*
Authors: Incheol Kang, Adam Lazere, Hendrikje Nienborg

The authors demonstrate that deep convolutional neural network (CNN) models of the primary visual cortex (V1) can be substantially simplified without loss of predictive power. By recording responses from over 29,000 neurons in mouse V1 and analyzing existing monkey datasets, they show that a two-layer model can capture more than 70% of response variance. They introduce "minimodels" that fit individual neurons using shared lower-level filters, showing that these simplified architectures provide a more interpretable account of how biological neurons achieve stimulus invariance and complex feature selectivity compared to overparameterized deep networks.
