# Ipek Oruc

*Visual recognition and neuroimaging* — University of British Columbia, Department of Ophthalmology and Visual Sciences, NOVA Lab.

## Background

Ipek Oruc is an Associate Professor at the University of British Columbia and director of the NOVA Lab, where they investigate the neural mechanisms of higher-level vision and object recognition. Oruc’s research integrates computer science, visual psychophysics, and neuroimaging to examine how the brain develops perceptual expertise for complex stimuli such as faces. They also study visual dysfunction in neurodevelopmental disorders like autism and conditions such as prosopagnosia to inform the development of rehabilitation paradigms. Additionally, Oruc applies deep learning and data science to create automated diagnostic tools and predictive models using retinal imaging.

## Papers

### 2025 — The blur paradox: Better recognition at a distance
*Journal of Vision*
Authors: Caitlin Long, Lei Yuan, Claudia Wu, Ipek Oruc

Under conditions of significant visual degradation, specifically blur, an inverse relationship exists between image size and recognition accuracy, where smaller or more distant stimuli are more easily identified. This investigation utilized a face recognition protocol with high-resolution grayscale celebrity images subjected to physical holographic filters to replicate this 'blur paradox' in a naturalistic viewing context. Across two experiments, participants demonstrated superior identification performance for blurry faces at smaller angular sizes (approximately 1.7°) compared to larger sizes (4.8°). The findings indicate that recognition efficiency improves gradually as viewing distance increases, suggesting that scale-dependent mechanisms in the visual system favor coarser, lower-spatial-frequency features when fine-scale information is obscured by blur.

### 2025 — AI-Assisted identification of sex-specific patterns in diabetic retinopathy using retinal fundus images
*PLOS ONE*
Authors: Parsa Delavari, Gulcenur Ozturan, Eduardo V. Navajas, Ozgur Yilmaz, Ipek Oruc

This research utilizes convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and explainable AI techniques to determine if diabetic retinopathy (DR) manifests with sexually dimorphic patterns in retinal fundus images. VGG16 models were fine-tuned on a curated dataset of nearly 3,000 images, matched for clinical confounders such as age, HbA1c levels, and disease severity. Saliency mapping via Guided Grad-CAM revealed that the diagnostic features identified by the AI varied by sex: classifications in female retinas were primarily driven by features in the macula, while male classifications relied on the optic disc and peripheral vasculature. These results suggest sex-dependent risk profiles for complications such as macular edema versus proliferative disease, potentially informing more personalized screening and diagnostic protocols.

### 2025 — CNN-Extracted Features Generate Synthetic fMRI Responses to Unseen Images
*Vision Research*
Authors: Parsa Delavari, Leonid Sigal, Ipek Oruc

To address the limitations imposed by the scarcity and high cost of human neuroimaging data, this study introduces a method for generating high-fidelity synthetic fMRI responses using features extracted from convolutional neural networks (CNNs). By establishing hierarchical correspondences between CNN layers and biological visual areas—such as the early visual cortex (EV) and the lateral occipital cortex (LOC)—the model produces voxel-wise activity patterns for novel visual stimuli. Decoding experiments demonstrated that these synthetic datasets can effectively augment real fMRI data, achieving superior performance in image classification and reconstruction tasks. The high correlation between generated and observed responses supports the utility of CNN-based augmentation for advancing computational models of the human visual system.

### 2024 — Atypical daily visual exposure to faces in adults with autism spectrum disorder
*Current Biology*
Authors: Todd Kamensek, Grace Iarocci, Ipek Oruc

This study provides the first longitudinal empirical measurement of the 'face diet'—daily visual exposure to faces—in adults with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) using head-mounted cameras. Analysis of over 360 hours of first-person perspective footage revealed that individuals with ASD experience a 30% reduction in total face exposure compared to neurotypical controls. This deficit is primarily characterized by significantly less time spent viewing familiar faces. Furthermore, the qualitative nature of face viewing in ASD is atypical, involving greater viewing distances (resulting in smaller retinal images) and a bias toward profile views rather than frontal orientations. These results suggest that the development of face-processing expertise in ASD may be constrained by both a quantitative lack of exposure and a qualitatively distinct visual environment.
