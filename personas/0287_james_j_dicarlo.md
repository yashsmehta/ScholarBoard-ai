# James J. DiCarlo

*visual object recognition and computational neuroscience* — MIT, Department of Brain and Cognitive Sciences, The DiCarlo Lab.

## Background

James J. DiCarlo is the Peter de Florez Professor of Neuroscience at MIT and an Investigator at the McGovern Institute for Brain Research. DiCarlo’s research focuses on reverse-engineering the neural mechanisms of human visual intelligence, specifically how the primate ventral visual stream enables object recognition. Through the integration of large-scale neurophysiology, machine learning, and neural perturbation, their lab develops neurally mechanistic computational models to explain how the brain transforms retinal images into invariant representations. DiCarlo also serves as the Director of the MIT Quest for Intelligence, coordinating interdisciplinary work at the intersection of natural and artificial intelligence.

## Papers

### 2025 — Noninvasive precision modulation of high-level neural population activity via natural vision perturbations
*arXiv*
Authors: Guy Gaziv, Sarah Goulding, Ani Ayvazian-Hancock, Yoon Bai, James J. DiCarlo

This study demonstrates that sensory-computable models of the primate ventral stream have reached a level of accuracy sufficient to drive predetermined activity patterns in high-level neural populations noninvasively. By applying perceptually subtle, model-guided perturbations to natural visual feeds, the researchers successfully modulated neural population activity in the macaque inferior temporal (IT) cortex. The experimental results showed a high quantitative alignment between the predicted neural effects and biological recordings, including the ability to inject experimenter-specified population patterns. These findings suggest that artificial neural network (ANN) models can now be used to design high-resolution, vision-based neural interventions that are potentially imperceptible to the subject.

### 2025 — Vision CNNs trained to estimate spatial latents learned similar ventral-stream-aligned representations
*International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR)*
Authors: Yudi Xie, Weichen Huang, Esther Alter, Jeremy Schwartz, Joshua B. Tenenbaum, James J. DiCarlo

Traditional modeling of the primate ventral stream has focused on object categorization as the primary optimization objective. This research explores the alternative hypothesis that the ventral stream may be optimized for estimating 3D spatial latents, such as object pose and position. Using synthetic datasets from a 3D graphics engine, the authors trained convolutional neural networks (CNNs) on various combinations of spatial and categorical tasks. They found that models optimized for only a few spatial latents achieved neural alignment scores with the primate ventral stream comparable to models trained on hundreds of object categories. The results indicate that internal representations for spatial estimation and categorization are highly similar, suggesting that the ventral stream's functional role may encompass a broader range of latent estimation tasks beyond simple recognition.

### 2024 — The Quest for an Integrated Set of Neural Mechanisms Underlying Object Recognition in Primates
*Annual Review of Vision Science*
Authors: Kohitij Kar, James J. DiCarlo

This review provides a comprehensive perspective on the current state of SMART (sensory computable, mechanistic, anatomically referenced, and testable) models of the primate ventral visual stream. The authors evaluate how leading artificial neural network (ANN) models align with biological brain activity and behavioral recognition patterns. The article discusses the transition from conceptual models to image-computable systems that can predict image-by-image neural responses in the inferior temporal (IT) cortex. It identifies key frontiers in the field, including the need for better integration of recurrent processing, topographic constraints, and the development of models that capture the full variance of primate visual intelligence.

### 2024 — Do Topographic Deep ANN Models of the Primate Ventral Stream Predict the Perceptual Effects of Direct IT Cortical Interventions?
*bioRxiv*
Authors: Martin Schrimpf, Paul McGrath, Eshed Margalit, James J. DiCarlo

Current topographic deep artificial neural network (TDANN) models have demonstrated the ability to capture the functional organization and response patterns of the primate ventral stream. This work extends these models by incorporating perturbation modules that simulate causal interventions such as microstimulation, optogenetic suppression, and muscimol inactivation. By evaluating these models against nine established benchmarks from the IT perturbation literature, the researchers found that TDANNs qualitatively predict the behavioral effects of neural interventions. However, the models currently exhibit quantitative misalignments, suggesting that while they are useful guides for causal hypothesis generation, further refinements in model architecture and topographic constraints are required for precise predictive accuracy.

### 2024 — Strong and Precise Modulation of Human Percepts via Robustified ANNs
*Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS) 36*
Authors: Guy Gaziv, Michael J. Lee, James J. DiCarlo

While standard artificial neural networks (ANNs) are sensitive to adversarial perturbations that do not affect human perception, this study demonstrates that robustified ANNs can guide the construction of low-norm image perturbations that strongly and precisely alter human category percepts. The researchers show that these robust models discover 'wormholes' in image space—subtle perturbations that shift a subject's perceptual state from one category to another semantically distinct category. By testing these perturbations on human observers, they found that contemporary ANN models of biological vision are now accurate enough to guide effective interventions in human perceptual states, bridging the gap between artificial and biological robustness.
