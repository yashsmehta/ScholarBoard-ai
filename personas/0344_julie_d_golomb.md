# Julie D. Golomb

*visual attention and perception* — The Ohio State University, Department of Psychology, Vision and Cognitive Neuroscience Lab.

## Background

Julie D. Golomb is a Professor at The Ohio State University and director of the Vision and Cognitive Neuroscience Lab, where they investigate how the brain constructs a stable perceptual representation from dynamic visual input. Golomb’s research employs behavioral psychophysics, functional MRI, EEG, and eye-tracking to explore interactions between attention, memory, and perception. Their work indicates that the visual system primarily encodes spatial location in retinotopic coordinates during high-level processing and examines how attention is remapped across eye movements. Current research focuses on the neural mechanisms of feature binding and the spatiotemporal representations of three-dimensional visual space.

## Papers

### 2025 — Spatiotemporal predictability of saccades modulates postsaccadic feature interference
*Journal of Vision*
Authors: Tzu-Yao Chiu, Isabel Jaen, Julie D. Golomb

This investigation explored whether the spatiotemporal predictability of saccadic eye movements mitigates the systematic feature interference and perceptual instability often observed during trans-saccadic remapping. Utilizing a postsaccadic feature report paradigm across two preregistered experiments, the authors manipulated the spatial and temporal certainty of upcoming saccades. While both spatial and temporal predictability enhanced overall task performance, spatial predictability specifically reduced 'swapping errors'—instances where participants erroneously reported features from the previous retinotopic location instead of the spatiotopic target. These findings demonstrate that high-level expectations regarding saccade targets facilitate the efficient remapping of spatial attention and the suppression of residual retinotopic traces, thereby maintaining perceptual stability across eye movements.

### 2025 — Post-saccadic disruption of semantic category information in naturalistic scenes
*bioRxiv (Preprint)*
Authors: Yong Min Choi, Tzu-Yao Chiu, Julie D. Golomb

This study examines the impact of saccadic eye movements on the neural and behavioral processing of high-level semantic category information in complex naturalistic scenes. Utilizing a combination of psychophysical categorization tasks and fMRI multivoxel pattern analysis (MVPA), the researchers assessed scene processing at varying post-saccadic latencies. Behavioral results indicated a robust decline in categorization accuracy for images presented within 50 ms after saccade completion. Corresponding fMRI data revealed that neural representational integrity for scene categories in the parahippocampal place area (PPA) was significantly degraded during short post-saccadic intervals (0–100 ms) compared to longer delays (400–600 ms). These findings suggest that the transient disruption of visual processing following a saccade extends beyond low-level features to impair high-level semantic representations in the ventral stream.

### 2025 — Unfolding spatiotemporal representations of 3D visual perception in the human brain
*bioRxiv (Preprint)*
Authors: Zitong Lu, Julie D. Golomb

This research investigates the neurocomputational mechanisms underlying the transformation of two-dimensional retinal inputs into three-dimensional spatial percepts. Employing a large-scale multimodal neuroimaging dataset (EEG and fMRI fusion) and representational similarity analysis (RSA), the authors characterized the spatiotemporal dynamics of multiple spatial features and coordinate systems, including Cartesian and Polar frameworks. While robust 2D spatial representations were detected early in the visual hierarchy, unique neural signatures for depth and integrated 3D features—such as 3D radius and 3D polar angle—emerged at later latencies and in higher-level cortical regions, including the parahippocampus. These results delineate a clear spatiotemporal trajectory of 3D spatial encoding, progressing from initial 2D mapping to complex, integrated 3D representations that support navigation and interaction.

### 2024 — Achieving more human brain-like vision via human EEG representational alignment
*Communications Biology*
Authors: Zitong Lu, Yile Wang, Julie D. Golomb

This study introduces Re(presentational)Al(ignment)net, a computational framework designed to enhance the neural alignment of computer vision models with human visual processing using non-invasive EEG recordings. By implementing a novel multi-layer encoding-based alignment paradigm, the authors modified the internal representations of the CORnet architecture to better approximate human neural activity patterns. Results demonstrate that ReAlnet significantly increases representational similarity to human EEG signals across diverse object categories and generalizes to unseen classes. Furthermore, cross-modal validation with fMRI data confirmed that these EEG-aligned models more closely resemble human cortical processing in multiple regions of interest (ROIs) compared to baseline models, providing a scalable method for developing personalized and brain-like artificial intelligence systems.

### 2024 — Human EEG and artificial neural networks reveal disentangled representations of object real-world size in natural images
*eLife*
Authors: Zitong Lu, Julie D. Golomb

This study utilizes representational similarity analysis on the large-scale THINGS-EEG2 dataset and various artificial neural network (ANN) architectures to disentangle the neural encoding of object real-world size from retinal size and perceived depth. The researchers identified a pure, unconfounded representation of real-world size in the human brain that emerges at late temporal stages, following the processing of depth and retinal size. Comparable findings were observed across diverse ANNs (ResNet, CLIP, and Word2Vec), where real-world size information was predominantly represented in deeper, higher-level layers. These results suggest that real-world size serves as a stable and high-level dimension of object space that integrates both visual and semantic information across biological and artificial systems.
