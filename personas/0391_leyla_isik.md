# Leyla Isik

*Computational cognitive neuroscience* — Johns Hopkins University, Department of Cognitive Science, Isik JHU Computational Cognitive Neuroscience Lab.

## Background

Leyla Isik is the Clare Boothe Luce Assistant Professor of Cognitive Science at Johns Hopkins University and directs a research program on the neural and computational basis of social vision. Isik's work examines how the human brain converts visual input into social representations, including the recognition of actions and complex social interactions. They employ a multi-disciplinary approach involving human neuroimaging with fMRI and MEG, behavioral psychophysics, and computational modeling such as deep neural networks and graph-based AI. Current research directions include studying the development of social perception in neurotypical and autistic populations and creating artificial intelligence systems designed to emulate human social intelligence.

## Papers

### 2025 — Early Neural Development of Social Interaction Perception: Evidence from Voxel-Wise Encoding in Young Children and Adults
*Journal of Neuroscience*
Authors: Elizabeth Jiwon Im, Angira Shirahatti, Leyla Isik

This study employs fMRI and voxel-wise encoding models to characterize the cortical maturation of social interaction perception in a developmental cohort of 122 children (ages 3–12) and 33 adults during naturalistic movie viewing. The movie was annotated with spatiotemporal features and high-level social categories, including motion energy, face presence, social interactions, and Theory of Mind (ToM) events. Results demonstrate that encoding models based on both low-level visual and high-level social features accurately predict brain activity in children as young as 3 years old. Crucially, social interaction representations in the superior temporal sulcus (STS) were found to be present early in childhood and attained adult-like functional profiles by age 7. These findings suggest that the specialized neural architecture for third-party social interaction perception develops rapidly and concurrently with basic visual systems like motion and face processing.

### 2025 — Modeling dynamic social vision highlights gaps between deep learning and humans
*International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR)*
Authors: Kathy Garcia, Emalie McMahon, Colin Conwell, Michael F. Bonner, Leyla Isik

This research benchmarks over 350 deep learning architectures—including image, video, and language-aligned models—against human behavioral ratings and fMRI responses to naturalistic videos of complex multi-agent social interactions. While state-of-the-art vision models reach the noise ceiling in predicting ventral stream responses and low-level scene features, they exhibit significant deficiencies in matching human social interaction judgments and neural activity within the lateral visual stream. Although video-based models show an advantage in mid-level lateral regions and language models (leveraging human-annotated captions) better align with behavioral ratings, all tested models fail to capture the neural representations of dynamic social vision. The results identify a substantial representational gap in AI's ability to model the hierarchical processing and abstract representations required for dynamic social perception.

### 2025 — Relational Information Predicts Human Behavior and Neural Responses to Complex Social Scenes
*Proceedings of the Annual Meeting of the Cognitive Science Society*
Authors: Wenshuo Qin, Manasi Malik, Leyla Isik

Tracking relational visual cues is essential for human social scene understanding, yet these cues are often underutilized in computational models. This study evaluates two graph-based models—SocialGNN and RNN Edge—that explicitly incorporate relational signals such as gaze direction and physical contact. Benchmarking these models against human behavioral ratings and fMRI data from naturalistic and animated stimuli (PHASE dataset), the authors found that both models effectively predict social interaction judgments. Notably, the simplified RNN Edge model, which processes only relational temporal edges without node features, achieved performance comparable to much larger state-of-the-art vision transformers. Neurally, these relational models significantly outperformed node-centric models in predicting responses in the superior temporal sulcus (STS), providing evidence that the STS primarily represents structured relational information during social perception.

### 2025 — A spatiotemporal hierarchy for social interaction perception in the lateral visual stream
*PsyArXiv*
Authors: Emalie McMahon, Elizabeth Jiwon Im, Michael F. Bonner, Leyla Isik

To characterize the spatiotemporal dynamics of social interaction perception, this study employs a regression-based fusion of fMRI and EEG data recorded during naturalistic movie viewing. The results delineate a functional hierarchy in the lateral visual stream, where posterior regions in the lateral occipitotemporal cortex (LOTC) process mid-level social action features and anterior regions in the superior temporal sulcus (STS) process increasingly abstract social content. Unlike the ventral stream's strict sequential processing, the lateral stream displays rapid, concurrent representation of both mid- and high-level features in anterior regions, with latencies consistent with rapid hierarchical computation. This study provides a comprehensive spatiotemporal map of the lateral social stream, suggesting it is optimized for high-speed, parallel processing of dynamic social relations.

### 2024 — Disentangled deep generative models reveal coding principles of the human face processing network
*PLOS Computational Biology*
Authors: Paul Soulos, Jean Daunizeau, Leyla Isik

This study investigates the computational principles of the human face processing network by testing the hypothesis that it employs a disentangled representation. Using deep generative models (Beta-VAE) to learn latent axes of variation from face images, the authors compared these representations to fMRI activity in the Occipital Face Area (OFA) and Fusiform Face Area (FFA). Encoding models based on disentangled latent variables significantly outperformed entangled ones, revealing that neural populations in the face network align with specific, statistically independent axes of facial variation. These results suggest that disentanglement serves as an organizing principle for high-level visual representations, enabling the brain to encode complex category-specific information through a structured and interpretable latent space.
