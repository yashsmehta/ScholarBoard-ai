# Marieke Mur

*Visual cognition and computational modeling* — Western University, Department of Psychology; Department of Computer Science; Brain and Mind Institute, Mur Lab.

## Background

Mur is an Assistant Professor at Western University with joint appointments in the Department of Psychology and the Department of Computer Science. Their research integrates psychology, neuroscience, and artificial intelligence to study how the human brain transforms visual input into representations. Using high-field functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), psychophysics, and deep neural networks, Mur investigates the neural population codes and representational geometries that support object recognition. Mur contributed to the development of representational similarity analysis (RSA), a framework used to connect brain-activity patterns, computational models, and behavioral measures.

## Papers

### 2025 — Toward task mapping of primate prefrontal cortex
*Neuropsychologia*
Authors: Jinkang Derrick Xiang, Julio C. Martinez-Trujillo, Taylor W. Schmitz, Marieke Mur

The lateral prefrontal cortex (LPFC) is characterized by its capacity to underpin a diverse array of cognitive functions, including inhibitory control, decision-making, and working memory. In contrast to neurons within sensory cortical regions, LPFC units demonstrate adaptive coding, where neural tuning dynamically reconfigures to represent task-relevant information across various domains. Such context-dependent responsiveness presents a significant challenge to conventional functional mapping methodologies. This article proposes a paradigm shift from stimulus-based mapping to 'task mapping' in the LPFC. We discuss the inherent structural and functional complexities of this region, such as integrative connectivity, dynamic tuning properties, and expansive spatiotemporal receptive fields. To address these, we introduce Topographic Similarity Analysis (TSA)—a framework derived from representational similarity analysis (RSA)—designed to detect and characterize functional topographies. We further discuss the capacity of TSA to identify the reorganization of neural topographies in response to learning and shifting task demands, and its potential extension to human high-field fMRI to delineate the cognitive dimensions of LPFC function.

### 2025 — Attentional modulation of stimulus-synchronized BOLD oscillations in the human visual cortex
*Journal of Vision*
Authors: Reebal W. Rafeh, Geoffrey N. Ngo, Lyle Muller, Ali R. Khan, Ravi S. Menon, Taylor W. Schmitz, Marieke Mur

Visual cortical neurons synchronize their firing rates to periodic visual stimuli. EEG is commonly used to study directed attention by frequency-tagging brain responses to multiple stimuli oscillating at different frequencies but is limited by its coarse spatial resolution. Here we leverage frequency-tagged fMRI (ft-fMRI) to study the influence of directed attention on the fine-grained spatiotemporal dynamics of competing stimulus-driven visual cortical oscillations. In this 7T fMRI experiment, participants (n=7) distributed their attention to simultaneously presented visual checkerboard stimuli oscillating at 0.125 Hz and 0.2 Hz. Our analysis revealed that distinct populations of visual cortical neurons exhibited either in-phase or anti-phase synchronization with the oscillating stimuli. The spatial topographies of these populations were highly replicable across scan sessions within participants, indicative of a fine-grained map of competitive feature-tuned responses (Dice's coefficient > 0.59; K-S test: p < 10-39). In accordance with this observation, we found that directed attention homogeneously increased the amplitude of anti-phase BOLD oscillations across the visual hierarchy, consistent with a distributed attention-driven suppressive field (Reynolds & Heeger, 2009). In contrast, attentional modulation of in-phase BOLD oscillations increased hierarchically from V1 to hV4, consistent with the effects of target enhancement reported in prior monkey electrophysiology (Moran & Desimone, 1985) and event-related fMRI work (Kastner et al., 1998). Finally, the strength of anti-phase (Wilcoxon test: p = 0.016), but not in-phase (p = 0.16), modulation predicted psychophysical correlates of attentional performance, further highlighting the mechanistic dissociation of attention-driven target enhancement and surround suppression. Together, our findings support the biased competition model of attention, which posits that attention modulates competing neural populations through concurrent mechanisms of enhancement and suppression. ft-fMRI extends the boundaries for research on the neural basis of biased competition in humans by providing a non-invasive method for distinguishing between concurrent stimulus-synchronized in-phase (enhancing) and anti-phase (suppressing) BOLD oscillations.

### 2024 — Frequency-tagged fMRI: A platform for fine-grained spatiotemporal analysis of cortical function
*bioRxiv*
Authors: Geoffrey N. Ngo, Reebal W. Rafeh, Lyle E. Muller, Ali R. Khan, Ravi S. Menon, Taylor W. Schmitz, Marieke Mur

We introduce and validate frequency-tagged functional MRI (ft-fMRI) as a high-resolution technique for mapping neural dynamics by synchronizing oscillatory stimuli with stimulus-driven BOLD responses. Utilizing a dual-frequency tagging protocol at 3T and 7T, we dissociated fundamental, multiplexed, and nonlinear intermodulation frequency responses within the human visual cortex at the level of individual vertices. The analysis revealed robust temporal synchronization and fine-grained cortical topographies. Specifically, we identified spatially dissociable populations of vertices that encoded multiple frequencies simultaneously (multiplexed) and others exhibiting nonlinear intermodulation components, showing reproducible dynamics across experimental sessions. By bridging the spatiotemporal resolution gap between traditional fMRI and electrophysiology, ft-fMRI provides a powerful platform for investigating fine-grained cortical computations underlying perception and multisensory integration.

### 2024 — The face inversion effect through the lens of deep neural networks
*Proceedings of the Royal Society B: Biological Sciences*
Authors: Ehsan Tousi, Marieke Mur

This work evaluates the longstanding debate regarding whether the face inversion effect (FIE) is the result of domain-specific processing mechanisms specialized for faces or domain-general mechanisms associated with perceptual expertise in individuating stimuli. Using deep neural networks (DNNs) as computational models, specifically VGG-16, we examined the impact of different training regimes on the emergence of inversion effects. By training networks to individuate either faces or non-face objects (e.g., birds), we analyzed the similarity of representations in the penultimate layer for upright versus inverted images. The results demonstrate that face-like inversion effects observed for objects of expertise, traditionally cited as evidence for domain-general accounts, can arise from domain-specific optimization. The findings suggest that the behavioral signatures of human face perception are well-explained by optimization for individuation tasks and that the underlying computations may not be unique to the face domain.

### 2024 — Task-specific topographic maps of neural activity in the primate lateral prefrontal cortex
*bioRxiv*
Authors: Jinkang Derrick Xiang, Megan Roussy, Benjamin Corrigan, Roberto A. Gulli, Rogelio Luna, Maryam Hasanzadeh Mofrad, Lyle Muller, Jörn Diedrichsen, Taylor W. Schmitz, Julio Martinez-Trujillo, Marieke Mur

The functional organization of the lateral prefrontal cortex (LPFC) remains poorly understood, particularly regarding whether activity topographies are stable or adapt to varying task demands. We characterized the functional topography of LPFC neural activity in rhesus macaques using chronically implanted multi-electrode arrays while the animals performed three distinct cognitive tasks: oculomotor delayed response (ODR), visuospatial working memory (VWM), and context-dependent decision making (CDM). We applied Topographic Similarity Analysis (TSA) to quantify the similarity of task tuning across electrodes. Our findings reveal that functional maps of neural population activity are remarkably stable within a given task across multiple sessions but adapt dynamically between different tasks. The topography exhibited a spatial scale consistent with afferent LPFC input patterns and cortical columns. These results indicate that LPFC topographic maps provide robust neural codes for task specialization while maintaining the flexibility to reorganize for different cognitive requirements.
