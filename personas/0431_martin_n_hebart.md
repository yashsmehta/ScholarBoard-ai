# Martin N. Hebart

*computational cognitive neuroscience* — Max Planck Institute for Human Cognitive and Brain Sciences, Department of Medicine and Independent Research Group Vision and Computational Cognition, Vision and Computational Cognition (ViCCo) Group.

## Background

Martin N. Hebart is a computational neuroscientist who researches the latent representational dimensions underlying human object recognition and semantic knowledge. Hebart directs the Vision and Computational Cognition group and participates in the THINGS initiative, utilizing large-scale behavioral and neuroimaging datasets to map multidimensional spaces of visual perception. Their methodology integrates multivariate pattern analysis and representational alignment techniques to compare human neural representations with deep neural networks and artificial intelligence. Hebart’s current research investigates the transformational cascade from low-level visual input to high-level semantic reasoning and how these processes are altered in psychiatric conditions involving visual phenomena.

## Papers

### 2025 — Predicting functional topography of the human visual cortex from cortical anatomy at scale
*bioRxiv*
Authors: Fernanda L. Ribeiro, Robert Satzger, Felix Hoffstaedter, Christian Bürger, Peer Herholz, David Linhardt, Noah C. Benson, D. Samuel Schwarzkopf, Alexander M. Puckett, Steffen Bollmann, Martin N. Hebart

The authors developed the deepRetinotopy toolbox, a geometric deep learning framework that predicts individual-specific functional topographic organization of the human visual cortex solely from structural MRI (T1-weighted) scans. Topographic mapping traditionally requires resource-intensive functional neuroimaging, but this model provides an efficient alternative that generates accurate retinotopic predictions (eccentricity and polar angle) across diverse datasets, scanners, and protocols. The utility of the predicted maps was demonstrated by automating individual visual area boundary delineation and by analyzing age-related changes in V1 organization across a sample of 11,060 anatomical scans, showing that large-scale functional organization is predictable from cortical anatomy alone.

### 2025 — Dimensions underlying the representational alignment of deep neural networks with humans
*Nature Machine Intelligence · 34 citations*
Authors: Florian P. Mahner, Lukas Muttenthaler, Umut Güçlü, Martin N. Hebart

This study investigates the latent factors determining the representational alignment between deep neural networks (DNNs) and human mental representations using a shared-dimension comparison framework. While global scalar metrics like Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA) quantify alignment levels, they fail to specify the underlying features driving this similarity. By mapping DNN activations and human behavioral judgments onto a 49-dimensional interpretable space, the researchers identified a fundamental divergence in representational strategies: human judgments are predominantly organized by semantic and conceptual dimensions, whereas DNNs exhibit a heavy reliance on low-level visual properties. These findings emphasize that high predictive accuracy in DNNs does not guarantee human-like semantic processing and highlight the necessity for dimension-specific alignment evaluations.

### 2025 — A high-throughput approach for the efficient prediction of perceived similarity of natural objects
*eLife · 9 citations*
Authors: Philipp Kaniuth, Florian P. Mahner, Jonas Perkuhn, Martin N. Hebart

The researchers present DimPred, a computational framework designed for high-throughput prediction of perceived object similarity from neural network activations. To overcome the combinatorial explosion of collecting human similarity judgments for large datasets, the approach maps DNN features onto 49 behaviorally-derived, interpretable representational dimensions using an L2-penalized regression model. Validation across five independent image sets showed that DimPred predicts behavioral ground-truth similarity with accuracies up to 0.898, consistently outperforming traditional RSA and feature-reweighted RSA methods. The results demonstrate that existing DNN representations are sufficient for automated sampling of behaviorally relevant similarity scores, facilitating large-scale alignment between computational models and human perception.

### 2025 — Decoding peripheral saccade targets from foveal retinotopic cortex
*eLife · 2 citations*
Authors: Luca Kämmer, Lisa M. Kroell, Tomas Knapen, Martin Rolfs, Martin N. Hebart

This study provides direct evidence of image-specific foveal prediction signals reaching the primary visual cortex (V1) during saccade preparation. Utilizing a gaze-contingent fMRI paradigm where peripheral saccade targets were removed before fixation, the authors demonstrated that stimulus identity could be decoded from the foveal retinotopic cortex despite the absence of direct foveal stimulation. Decoding sensitivity was specific to stimulus shape but not semantic category, suggesting a low-level feature-based feedback mechanism. Furthermore, fluctuations in foveal decodability covaried with activity in the intraparietal sulcus, identifying it as a candidate source for the feedback. These findings reveal a neural mechanism for trans-saccadic perceptual stability where foveal regions predictively represent upcoming visual inputs.

### 2025 — The scope and limits of fine-grained image and category information in the ventral visual pathway
*Journal of Neuroscience · 5 citations*
Authors: Markus W. Badwal, Johanna Bergmann, Johannes H.R. Roth, Christian F. Doeller, Martin N. Hebart

The authors characterize the cortical organization of coarse-scale category versus fine-grained image-specific information in the human ventral visual stream using high-resolution fMRI and the large-scale THINGS database. Applying multivariate pattern analysis (MVPA) to neural responses from 8,000 images, they identified distinct representational structures for semantic categories and individual exemplars. While category-selective regions (e.g., face- or place-selective clusters) exhibit clear domain-specific preferences, the study found they also encode extensive fine-grained information about individual images within and across categories. These results suggest a multidimensional representational architecture in high-level visual cortex where specialized modules are integrated into a broader, continuous representational landscape supporting diverse behavioral goals.
