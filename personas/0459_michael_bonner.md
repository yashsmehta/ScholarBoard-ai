# Michael Bonner

*computational neuroscience and deep learning* — Johns Hopkins University, Department of Cognitive Science, Bonner Lab.

## Background

Michael F. Bonner is an Assistant Professor in the Department of Cognitive Science at Johns Hopkins University, where he directs the Bonner Lab. His research investigates the computational principles of the human visual system by integrating functional neuroimaging with deep neural network modeling. He focuses on characterizing the high-dimensional nature of cortical representations, challenging traditional low-dimensional theories of neural coding through advanced statistical techniques. His recent work explores the alignment between artificial and biological vision systems and the latent manifold structure that underlies individual differences in visual experience.

## Papers

### 2026 — High-dimensional structure underlying individual differences in naturalistic visual experience
*Current Biology · 3 citations*
Authors: Chihye Han, Michael F. Bonner

The research investigates the neural mechanisms by which unique subjective visual experiences are constructed from shared sensory inputs by analyzing the high-dimensional geometry of the human visual hierarchy. Utilizing spectral decomposition of fMRI responses acquired during naturalistic movie viewing, the authors demonstrate that idiosyncratic neural signatures are robustly maintained across several orders of magnitude of the latent eigenspectrum. This multidimensional representation, which is not captured by standard intersubject correlation (ISC) metrics, significantly predicts individual variance in subsequent memory recall, including the semantic richness and abstraction of narrative descriptions. The findings suggest that individual perception is mediated by information integration across an expansive, high-dimensional manifold in the visual cortex.

### 2025 — Universal scale-free representations in human visual cortex
*PLOS Computational Biology · 13 citations*
Authors: Raj Magesh Gauthaman, Brice Ménard, Michael F. Bonner

Utilizing the Natural Scenes Dataset, this study characterizes the large-scale population covariance of the human visual cortex to identify its fundamental organizing principles. By applying a spectral analysis to fMRI responses, the authors discovered that neural representations follow a consistent scale-free organization, with variance decaying as a power law across approximately four orders of magnitude of latent dimensions. This scale-invariant structure is highly conserved across individual subjects and across multiple regions of the visual hierarchy from V1 to V4. Furthermore, functional hyperalignment reveals that these high-dimensional dimensions are shared between brains, suggesting a universal spectrum of visual information that is systematically distributed across the entire representational manifold rather than confined to low-dimensional subspaces.

### 2025 — Universal dimensions of visual representation
*Science Advances · 13 citations*
Authors: Zirui Chen, Michael F. Bonner

This work addresses whether the representational alignment between deep neural networks (DNNs) and the human visual cortex is driven by specific optimization constraints, such as architecture and task objectives, or by universal features of natural image representation. By characterizing the universality of latent dimensions across an ensemble of hundreds of networks with diverse designs, the authors identified a common subspace of shared representational features. Comparative analysis with human fMRI data showed that the specific features which align most closely with cortical responses are those that are universal across artificial networks, independent of their individual task or architectural biases. This suggests that biological and artificial vision systems converge on similar high-dimensional geometries dictated by the statistical regularities of natural visual inputs.

### 2025 — Convolutional architectures are cortex-aligned de novo
*Nature Machine Intelligence · 6 citations*
Authors: Atlas Kazemian, Eric Elmoznino, Michael F. Bonner

The study investigates the role of architectural inductive biases in the emergence of cortex-aligned representations in neural networks, challenging the prevailing view that massive experience-dependent training is necessary for brain-model similarity. The authors demonstrate that untrained convolutional neural networks (CNNs) exhibit significant representational alignment with the ventral visual streams of humans and macaques 'de novo,' provided they incorporate specific architectural constraints: spatial dimensionality compression via pooling and feature-channel expansion. In contrast, scaling feature dimensionality in transformers or fully connected architectures failed to generate similar brain-like representations. The results suggest that the fundamental wiring of the visual cortex is closely mimicked by the architectural constraints of CNNs, allowing for brain-aligned processing even without learning.

### 2024 — High-performing neural network models of visual cortex benefit from high latent dimensionality
*PLOS Computational Biology · 60 citations*
Authors: Eric Elmoznino, Michael F. Bonner

This study evaluates the 'compression hypothesis' in visual neuroscience, which posits that neural networks should reduce image representations to low-dimensional manifolds to achieve robust semantic invariance. By quantifying the latent dimensionality of numerous DNN models of the visual system, the authors demonstrate that, conversely, models with high effective dimensionality are superior predictors of neural activity in both human fMRI and macaque electrophysiology. Furthermore, high latent dimensionality was positively correlated with improved generalization performance when learning novel object categories. These results provide evidence that the visual cortex prioritizes high-dimensional sensory codes that preserve rich perceptual detail, challenging traditional views that emphasize low-dimensional representations as the goal of cortical visual processing.
