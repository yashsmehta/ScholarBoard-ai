# Michelle Greene

*Visual scene perception* — Barnard College, Department of Psychology, Greene Lab.

## Background

Michelle R. Greene is an Assistant Professor of Psychology at Barnard College who examines the neural and computational foundations of rapid visual scene perception. Greene’s research utilizes behavioral psychophysics, electroencephalography (EEG), and machine learning to study how humans and artificial intelligence systems extract semantic meaning from complex environments. Greene developed the Visual Experience Dataset—a head-mounted video and gaze-tracking corpus—and conducts NSF-funded research on the efficient coding of visual, structural, and conceptual scene features. Their current projects aim to bridge the gap between human visual cognition and computer vision models.

## Papers

### 2025 — Divergent roles of visual structure and conceptual meaning in scene detection and categorization
*Journal of Vision*
Authors: Sage Aronson, Maria S. Adkins, Michelle R. Greene

The rapid recognition of scene gist suggests an efficient visual processing mechanism, yet the factors determining the ease of scene understanding remain unclear. We investigate the dissociation between visual structure—quantified via image compressibility metrics—and conceptual richness—derived from human-generated semantic descriptions. Across four behavioral paradigms involving rapid scene detection and basic-level categorization, we demonstrate that high visual information content consistently impairs performance, indicative of a perceptual information bottleneck. Conversely, high semantic complexity selectively enhances early detection sensitivity (d') while having a minimal or nuanced impact on categorization accuracy. These results suggest a functional dissociation where structural complexity imposes processing costs, whereas semantic richness facilitates early perceptual access, possibly through top-down modulation of visual templates.

### 2025 — Look to the behavior—but first, define it: toward a behavioral taxonomy of vision
*Cognitive Neuroscience*
Authors: Michelle R. Greene

Current debates in visual neuroscience suggest that the functional organization of the occipitotemporal cortex is driven by behavioral relevance rather than rigid stimulus categories. However, the lack of a systematic behavioral taxonomy—an ontology of what humans actually do in real-world environments—limits the actionability of this hypothesis. I argue that the path forward requires leveraging naturalistic datasets that synchronize tasks, gaze tracking, and physical movement to identify the 'basic-level' actions of human behavior. By treating category-selectivity as a data-driven outcome of adaptive visual processing rather than a starting assumption, we can discover which ecological behaviors consistently recruit specific neural categorizations to support goal-directed actions.

### 2025 — Visual and Semantic Scene Information: A Steady-State Visual Evoked Potentials Study
*ResearchGate (Preprint)*
Authors: Skylar Stadhard, Sage Aronson, Michelle R. Greene

Efficient coding theories propose that the visual system compresses redundant information to optimize processing. This study utilizes steady-state visual evoked potentials (SSVEP) to assess how structural (visual) and conceptual (semantic) information levels modulate objective scene recognition. We presented triplets of images designed to maximize similarity in one information dimension while minimizing it in others. Our findings reveal that the brain's neural signature for scene information evolves over time, with early responses tracking low-level image entropy and later components aligning with semantic complexity. This temporal progression underscores how the sparse code for scenes is flexibly adapted to suit the behavioral goals and information capacity limits of the mammalian visual system.

### 2025 — Brain-guided Convolutional Neural Networks Reveal Task-Specific Representations in Scene Processing
*bioRxiv (Preprint)*
Authors: Bruce C. Hansen, Michelle R. Greene, Henry A. S. Lewinsohn, Audrey E. Kris, Sophie Smyth, Binghui Tang

Although scene categorization is the primary benchmark for visual understanding, human observers perform a multitude of tasks within any environment. To investigate how task demands reshape scene representations, we developed a novel brain-guided convolutional neural network (CNN) where each layer was guided by time-resolved ERP responses recorded during object detection and scene affordance tasks. The network successfully predicted task-specific differences by leveraging image features that humans identified as crucial for each goal. Our activation map analysis revealed that the brain's spatiotemporal representation of local features evolves systematically from 244 ms to 402 ms post-stimulus, demonstrating that distinct image features emerge at different processing stages depending on the observer's goals.

### 2024 — Learning Invariant Object Representations in Deep Convolutional Neural Networks
*Biorxiv (Preprint)*
Authors: William Davis, Michelle R. Greene

Deep convolutional neural networks (dCNNs) have achieved near-human accuracy in visual categorization, yet they often lack the robust viewpoint and scale invariance characterized by human vision. We evaluate the conditions under which dCNNs develop invariant object representations using a combination of synthetic and real-world image datasets. By analyzing the activation patterns across network layers, we investigate how the integration of diverse transformational data (the 'visual diet') affects the emergence of canonical versus non-canonical viewpoint sensitivity. Our results demonstrate that supervised training on ecologically representative viewpoints facilitates the development of representational geometries that mirror the human ventral stream, suggesting that environmental statistics are a primary driver of biological-like invariance in artificial systems.
