# Nihong Chen

*visual perception and plasticity* — Tsinghua University, Department of Psychology, School of Social Sciences, Chen Nihong's Research Group.

## Background

Nihong Chen is an Associate Professor at Tsinghua University and a Principal Investigator at the IDG/McGovern Institute for Brain Research. Chen’s research investigates the neural mechanisms of human visual perception, focusing on how perceptual learning and attention reshape the functional architecture of the visual cortex. Using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), and psychophysical modeling, they have characterized the cortical plasticity of areas like V3A and MT+ and explored the computational strategies the brain employs to resolve visual crowding. Chen’s current work examines how cortical population codes and multi-stage integration models facilitate object recognition in complex and ambiguous visual environments.

## Papers

### 2024 — Multivariate Patterns of fMRI Activity in Human V2 Predict Feature Binding of Color and Motion
*Neuroscience Bulletin*
Authors: Yan-Yu Zhang, Xilin Zhang, Nihong Chen

This study investigates the neural architecture of feature binding using a steady-state misbinding illusion where color and motion signals are perceived incorrectly relative to their physical properties. Utilizing 7-Tesla fMRI and multivariate pattern analysis (MVPA), the researchers examined whether the human visual cortex codes for physical or perceived feature pairings. The results demonstrate that multivariate patterns of activity in the secondary visual area (V2), but not V1, significantly predict the perceived (misbound) combinations of color and motion. Additionally, direction-selective adaptation effects in V2 were found to be contingent on color and significantly correlated with the magnitude of psychophysical aftereffects across subjects, indicating that V2 is a critical site for the active binding of basic visual features into coherent objects.

### 2024 — Seeing in crowds: Averaging first, then max
*Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*
Authors: Xincheng Lu, Ruijie Jiang, Meng Song, Yiting Wu, Yiran Ge, Nihong Chen

To delineate the pooling strategies underlying the visual crowding effect—a bottleneck in peripheral object recognition—this research measured the internal response distributions of human observers during an orientation discrimination task. In contrast to standard compulsory averaging models, human response patterns revealed that perceptual decisions under crowded conditions are better characterized by a non-linear two-stage model. This proposed 'Avg-Max' model posits an initial stage of local orientation signal averaging followed by a late-stage signed-max operation that selects the extreme response across items. These results suggest that the visual system employs a combination of linear integration and non-linear decision rules to optimize information extraction from cluttered peripheral environments.

### 2024 — Learning Improves Peripheral Vision Via Enhanced Cortico-Cortical Communications
*Neuroscience Bulletin*
Authors: Yuwei Cui, Xincheng Lu, MiYoung Kwon, Nihong Chen

This study explores the neural plasticity induced by Preferred Retinal Location (PRL) training in normally sighted adults, which is known to alleviate crowding and enhance peripheral visual resolution. Employing functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) combined with effective connectivity analysis (Dynamic Causal Modeling), the researchers investigated changes in inter-areal communication before and after training. The results demonstrate that perceptual learning significantly enhances feedforward connectivity from the primary visual cortex (V1) to extrastriate regions (V2 and V3) specifically at the trained PRL. These findings provide neurobiological support for a reweighting theory of perceptual learning, suggesting that the visual system adaptively prioritizes task-relevant signal transmission along the cortical hierarchy to overcome resolution bottlenecks.

### 2024 — A comparison of statistical learning of naturalistic textures between DCNNs and the human visual hierarchy
*Science China Technological Sciences*
Authors: Xincheng Lu, Ziqi Yuan, Yichi Zhang, Hailin Ai, Siyuan Cheng, Yiran Ge, Fang Fang, Nihong Chen

This study compares the acquisition of high-order statistical regularities in naturalistic textures between human observers and Deep Convolutional Neural Networks (DCNNs). Using a psychophysical paradigm that targets specific image statistics, the researchers found that DCNNs replicate the human-like learning curves and performance improvements across various texture families. Computational analysis of the model's internal representations reveals that texture selectivity enhancement is hierarchically distributed, with more pronounced plasticity occurring in deeper convolutional layers (L4 and L5). These findings suggest that DCNNs can serve as effective digital twins for investigating the neural mechanisms of statistical learning and the cortical organization of natural scene encoding in the primate ventral stream.

### 2024 — Attention spotlight in V1-based cortico-cortical interactions in human visual hierarchy
*Scientific Reports*
Authors: Yanyu Zhang, Xilin Zhang, Xincheng Lu, Nihong Chen

This investigation demonstrates the neural correlates of the 'attention spotlight' by analyzing the spatial distribution of signal transmission within the human visual cortex. Utilizing fMRI background connectivity analysis, the researchers mapped the interactions between retinotopic V1 and downstream areas (V2–V4) under focal and diffused attentional states. The data reveal that focal attention sharpens the spatial gradient of background connectivity, prioritizing signal transmission at attended locations relative to the surround. Dynamic Causal Modeling (DCM) further indicates that this attentional modulation involves both feedforward and feedback recurrent processing. These results characterize attention as a context-dependent filter that enhances the precision of information transmission across the early stages of the visual hierarchy.
