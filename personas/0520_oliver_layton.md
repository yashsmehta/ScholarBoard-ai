# Oliver Layton

*computational cognitive neuroscience* — Colby College, Computer Science, Optic Flow and Wayfinding Lab (OWLab).

## Background

Oliver W. Layton is an Associate Professor of Computer Science at Colby College. Their research focuses on the neural mechanisms underlying human navigation and self-motion perception, combining large-scale dynamical neural modeling with psychophysical experiments to investigate how the primate brain processes optic flow. Layton developed the Competitive Dynamics model to simulate interactions between cortical areas such as MSTd, MT, and V1, exploring how these systems facilitate figure-ground segregation and world-relative object motion recovery. Their current work evaluates the efficacy of accuracy-optimized convolutional neural networks in modeling the primate dorsal stream and applies bio-inspired algorithms to autonomous navigation.

## Papers

### 2026 — Artificial Neural Networks and the Visual System: A Brief History and Future Directions
*VISION (The Journal of the Vision Society of Japan)*
Authors: Oliver W. Layton

This review explores the historical and conceptual synergy between the architecture of artificial neural networks (ANNs) and the computational principles of the biological visual system. Over the past half-century, developments in ANN design have been deeply informed by neurophysiological findings, particularly the hierarchical organization of the primate visual cortex. The article traces this evolution from early models like the Neocognitron to contemporary convolutional neural networks (CNNs), which have demonstrated remarkable success in modeling response properties in the ventral stream. Conversely, the paper examines how these computational models now serve as powerful tools for generating testable hypotheses in visual neuroscience. Finally, the author discusses future directions for bridging the gap between biological vision and machine intelligence, emphasizing the potential for biologically inspired constraints to improve the robustness and interpretability of vision models.
