# Ratan Murty

*cognitive computational neuroscience* — Georgia Institute of Technology, School of Psychology, Murty Lab.

## Background

Apurva Ratan Murty is an Assistant Professor at the Georgia Institute of Technology and director of the Murty Lab, where they explore the intersection of human vision, cognitive science, and artificial intelligence. Murty's research program employs high-field functional MRI and deep convolutional neural networks to model the neural codes and algorithms that support visual perception and category selectivity. Their methodology involves developing biologically constrained encoding models of cortical organization that predict neural responses to novel visual stimuli. This work investigates the functional architecture of the ventral visual pathway and the neurobiological basis of perceptual abnormalities such as agnosias. Prior to this appointment, Murty completed postdoctoral fellowships at MIT's McGovern Institute and earned a PhD from the Indian Institute of Science.

## Papers

### 2025 — Targeted perturbations reveal brain-like local coding axes in robustified, but not standard, ANN-based brain models
*arXiv*
Authors: Nikolas McNeal, N. Apurva Ratan Murty

While artificial neural network (ANN)-based encoding models effectively predict neural responses, their reliance on overall predictive accuracy fails to distinguish between models with divergent representational geometries. This research employs small-scale adversarial probes to investigate the local representational manifolds of highly predictive brain models. Findings indicate that standard ANN-based encoding models are significantly fragile, exhibiting sensitivity to imperceptible perturbations and relying on unstable local coding directions that fail to transfer across architectures. In contrast, adversarial probes optimized against robustified models elicit semantically meaningful and generalizable shifts that align with the local coding dimensions of the biological visual system. The study establishes that local representational geometry serves as a more rigorous criterion for neural model evaluation and demonstrates that robust models better capture the underlying principles of neural selectivity.

### 2025 — TopoNets: High performing vision and language models with brain-like topography
*International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR)*
Authors: Mayukh Deb, Mainak Deb, N. Apurva Ratan Murty

Biological neural systems are characterized by functional topography, where neurons with similar response properties are spatially clustered. Standard deep learning architectures lack this organization, and previous attempts to induce topography often resulted in significant task performance degradation. This work introduces TopoLoss, a general-purpose inductive bias that enforces spatial smoothness on internal representations. By integrating TopoLoss into training, the authors develop TopoNets—a suite of topographic vision (ResNet, ViT) and language (GPT-Neo) models. TopoNets achieve state-of-the-art performance for topographic models on ImageNet and linguistic tasks while maintaining high alignment with human cortical topography. The models exhibit computational advantages, including parameter efficiency and lower effective dimensionality, successfully recapitulating the functional organization observed in human visual and language cortices.

### 2025 — End-to-end topographic auditory models replicate signatures of human auditory cortex
*arXiv*
Authors: Haider Al-Tahan, Mayukh Deb, Jenelle Feather, N. Apurva Ratan Murty

The human auditory cortex exhibits complex topographic organization, including smooth maps for tonotopy and amplitude modulation, alongside modular regions selective for music and speech. Current computational models of audition generally fail to capture this spatial structure. This study presents TopoAudio, the first end-to-end topographic auditory models developed by adapting wiring-length constraints to cochleagram-based deep neural networks. TopoAudio models achieve competitive accuracy in predicting human auditory fMRI responses and performing sound classification. Crucially, they naturally develop hallmark features of biological auditory organization, such as smooth tonotopic gradients in early layers and spatially clustered selectivity for music and speech in higher layers. These results suggest that spatial smoothness constraints are a foundational regularizer for achieving biologically aligned sensory representations across modalities.

### 2025 — MOSAIC: A scalable framework for fMRI dataset aggregation and modeling of human vision
*bioRxiv*
Authors: Benjamin Lahner, Mayukh Deb, N. Apurva Ratan Murty, Aude Oliva

Despite the utility of large-scale vision fMRI datasets, practical constraints often limit the number of participants and the diversity of stimuli within individual studies. This paper proposes MOSAIC, a framework designed for the scalable aggregation of functionally diverse fMRI datasets into a common representational space. By standardizing data from multiple sources, MOSAIC allows for the training of vision foundation models that benefit from vastly increased stimulus variety and participant numbers. The framework facilitates the evaluation of model-brain alignment across broad experimental conditions and enables the development of robust, cross-participant encoding models. This approach addresses current bottlenecks in cognitive computational neuroscience by providing a path toward foundation models of human visual representation.

### 2024 — Linking faces to social cognition: The temporal pole as a potential social switch
*Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS)*
Authors: Alish Dipani, Nikolas McNeal, N. Apurva Ratan Murty

Identifying the neural mechanisms that link face recognition to social understanding remains a central challenge in neuroscience. This article evaluates recent evidence demonstrating specialized face-selective regions in the human temporal pole (TP) and perirhinal cortex (PR). While the PR appears primarily involved in processing face identity through image-computable features, face responses in the TP are strongly modulated by familiarity. The findings support a model where the TP acts as a 'social switch,' linking recognized individuals to broader social networks for memory retrieval and personality judgment. This division of labor within the anterior face-processing network provides a mechanistic link between visual perception and social cognition, suggesting that familiar faces trigger unique social computations not captured by standard feed-forward vision models.
