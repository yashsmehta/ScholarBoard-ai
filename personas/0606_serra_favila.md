# Serra Favila

*cognitive neuroscience of episodic memory* — Brown University, Department of Cognitive, Linguistic, and Psychological Sciences, Favila Lab.

## Background

Serra Favila is an Assistant Professor at Brown University who researches the neural and computational mechanisms of human episodic memory. Favila examines how environmental contexts, task goals, and interactions between similar memories transform mental representations and influence behavior. Their research utilizes functional neuroimaging (fMRI), intracranial recordings, eye-tracking, and computational modeling. Favila studies how hippocampal differentiation and visual cortex reactivation resolve competition during memory-guided attention.

## Papers

### 2025 — Early visual cortex supports one-shot episodic memory via spatially tuned reactivation
*bioRxiv*
Authors: Robert Woodry, Jonathan Winawer, Serra Favila

This study examines the hypothesis that episodic memory retrieval is supported by the reactivation of perceptual traces in sensory cortex. While previous research has documented cortical reinstatement in early visual areas following extensive associative training, it remains a central question whether such neural responses occur after a single encoding event. Using high-resolution fMRI and population receptive field (pRF) modeling, we characterized spatially tuned memory responses in areas V1–V3 following one-shot encoding. Participants performed recognition and spatial recall tasks for objects presented once in the periphery. Results demonstrated low-amplitude, spatially tuned responses in early visual cortex that were precisely aligned with the encoded location, even during recognition trials where spatial memory was not explicitly required. Crucially, the fidelity and precision of these reactivated spatial representations were significantly higher for successfully remembered items than for forgotten ones, indicating that early sensory cortex maintains and reinstates detailed stimulus information from a single experience to support episodic retrieval.

### 2025 — Deep Vision Models Follow Shepard's Universal Law of Generalization
*Proceedings of the 47th Annual Meeting of the Cognitive Science Society*
Authors: Daniel L. Carstensen, Serra E. Favila, Steven M. Frankland

Shepard’s (1987) universal law of generalization posits that the probability of generalization between stimuli decays as a concave function of their distance in psychological space. While this law is a fundamental principle of biological perception, its validity in artificial neural networks (ANNs) has not been extensively characterized. We evaluated 26 diverse deep vision models by comparing their embedding space distances to human similarity judgments for naturalistic images. Using non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) to derive proxy psychological spaces, we found that internal representations across various model architectures and training paradigms robustly adhere to Shepard’s law, producing concave generalization gradients aligned with human psychological data. These findings suggest that modern deep vision systems naturally exploit representational geometries similar to those in human psychological space, with semantic features playing a critical role in facilitating this alignment.

### 2025 — The multiple roles of visual cortex in single-shot episodic memory: encoding, recognition, and recall
*Journal of Vision*
Authors: Robert Woodry, Jonathan Winawer, Serra E. Favila

Sensory cortex exhibits activity patterns during long-term memory retrieval similar to those during encoding. This memory reactivation in primary visual cortex is especially robust for items that were highly practiced in an associative learning task requiring repeated, precise judgments (Woodry, Curtis, Winawer, 2024). Here we examined the role of visual cortex in a more natural, one-shot memory paradigm. Building on a pilot fMRI study (n=5) by Favila and Winawer (VSS 2020), we increased the sample size (n=13) and conducted new analyses of an experiment in which participants encountered 480 object images across three interleaved tasks: encoding, recognition, and spatial recall. During encoding scans, peripheral objects appeared at one of four polar angles while subjects maintained central fixation and judged whether the object was bigger/smaller than a shoebox. During recognition scans, either the same objects or lures were presented centrally while subjects made old/new judgments. During spatial recall scans, subjects viewed old objects centrally and indicated the polar angle of the object at encoding. We observed spatially tuned responses in visual cortex, including V1, that were relevant for encoding success, recognition, and recall. First, at encoding, V1 responses were larger and more sharply tuned for objects whose location was successfully recalled later. Second, spatially tuned responses were evoked in visual cortex during successful recognition, even though the items had only been viewed once and location wasn’t probed. Moreover, spatial tuning in visual cortex during recognition was poor for objects later forgotten (incorrect spatial judgment), indicating that the spatially tuned recognition responses were behaviorally relevant. Third, when aligning to the reported location during recall, we observed spatially tuned responses for both remembered and forgotten objects. These findings show how the properties of visual cortex activation track memory behavior during encoding, recognition, and recall, even for single shot memory.

### 2023 — Hippocampal mechanisms resolve competition in memory and perception
*bioRxiv (Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory) · 8 citations*
Authors: Serra E. Favila, Mariam Aly

Behaving adaptively requires selection of relevant memories and sensations and suppression of competing ones. We hypothesized that these mechanisms are linked, such that hippocampal computations that resolve competition in memory also shape the precision of sensory representations to guide selective attention. We leveraged fMRI-based pattern similarity, receptive field modeling, and eye tracking to test this hypothesis in humans performing a memory-dependent visual search task. In the hippocampus, differentiation of competing memories predicted the accuracy of memory-guided eye movements. In visual cortex, preparatory coding of remembered target locations predicted search successes, whereas preparatory coding of competing locations predicted search failures due to interference. These effects were linked: stronger hippocampal memory differentiation was associated with lower competitor activation in visual cortex, yielding more precise preparatory representations. These results demonstrate a role for memory differentiation in shaping the precision of sensory representations, highlighting links between mechanisms that overcome competition in memory and perception.

### 2023 — Hippocampal and visual cortex contributions to resolving competition during memory-guided attention
*Journal of Vision*
Authors: Serra E. Favila, Mariam Aly

Memory can guide attention, improving our ability to quickly identify or detect visual stimuli in complex environments. However, many of our memories contain overlapping features, which can trigger them to compete in guiding behavior. How does the brain overcome memory competition to effectively guide visual attention? We designed a memory-guided attention task that required human participants to recall scene-location memories to predict the location of search targets (small visual distortions) on the following trial. Critically, all scenes had a visually similar, and therefore competing, scene that guided search to a different location. Analysis of eye movements confirmed that participants effectively used memory to improve visual search; search was more accurate on trials for which memory-based predictions were valid, compared to when they were invalid or when no memory was available. However, participants also made some eye movements to the competing location, suggesting that interference between memories was also present. Using fMRI, we tested the hypothesis that the hippocampus would differentiate competing memories, allowing visual cortex to anticipate the correct target location and guide precise eye movements. Hippocampal activity patterns for competing memories were less correlated than activity patterns for unrelated memories, and stronger hippocampal memory differentiation was related to better visual search. We then used a population receptive field model fit to independent data to evaluate spatially-selective activity in visual cortex and its relationship to subsequent eye movements during search. Anticipatory activation of the target location in visual cortex predicted accurate search performance on the next trial, whereas activation of the competing location predicted poor performance. Ongoing analyses aim to directly relate memory differentiation in the hippocampus to spatial anticipation in visual cortex. These results suggest that the hippocampus may resolve competition between similar memories, enabling visual cortex to anticipate task-relevant locations to prepare efficient behavior.
