# Kenneth Harris

*quantitative neuroscience and neural coding* — University College London, UCL Queen Square Institute of Neurology, Cortical Processing Laboratory (Cortexlab).

## Background

Kenneth Harris is a Professor of Quantitative Neuroscience at University College London and co-directs the Cortical Processing Laboratory. Harris researches how large-scale populations of neurons in the cerebral cortex integrate sensory information with internal brain states to guide perceptual decisions. Their work in neurotechnology and data analysis includes the development of Neuropixels probes and Kilosort spike-sorting software. These tools are used for the high-density recording and characterization of thousands of individual neurons across multiple brain regions.

## Papers

### 2026 — High-dimensional neuronal activity from low-dimensional latent dynamics: a solvable model
*NeurIPS 2025 (Oral) / bioRxiv*
Authors: Valentin Schmutz, Ali Haydaroğlu, Shuqi Wang, Yixiao Feng, Matteo Carandini, Kenneth D. Harris

This work addresses the paradox between the theoretical hypothesis of low-dimensional latent dynamics in recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and experimental observations of high-dimensional neural population activity in the mammalian cortex. The authors present an analytically solvable RNN model where low-dimensional pre-activation dynamics generate high-dimensional post-activation activity through nonlinear neuronal activation functions, producing power-law covariance eigenspectra consistent with cortical data. They introduce the 'Neural Cross-Encoder' (NCE), a nonlinear latent variable identification method, to infer pre-activation dimensionality. Applying NCE to large-scale Neuropixels recordings from the mouse primary visual cortex, they find that while responses to drifting gratings and spontaneous activity are consistent with low-dimensional latents, responses to naturalistic stimuli remain high-dimensional even at the latent level.

### 2025 — AIRUS: a simple workflow for AI-assisted exploration of scientific data
*bioRxiv*
Authors: Kenneth D. Harris

This methodological note proposes AIRUS (AI Research Under Supervision), an iterative and accessible workflow leveraging 'reasoning' large language models (LLMs) to facilitate scientific data analysis. The framework enables researchers to utilize LLMs for generating scientific hypotheses, producing executable analysis code, interpreting computational results, and refining analytical approaches in successive cycles. The author demonstrates the efficacy of this human-in-the-loop AI system through two case studies: an analysis of synthetic datasets and a complex exploration of real-world electrophysiological data from the International Brain Laboratory. The workflow is designed to empower experimentalists lacking deep programming or AI expertise to perform sophisticated data exploration via a simple copy-paste interface between LLMs and cloud-based notebooks.

### 2025 — Functional Dissociation of Layers 2/3 and 5 in the Primary Visual Cortex
*bioRxiv*
Authors: Timothy P.H. Sit, Célian Bimbard, Anna Lebedeva, Matteo Carandini, Philip Coen, Kenneth D. Harris

Utilizing large-scale two-photon calcium imaging and high-density Neuropixels recordings, this study challenges the classical columnar hypothesis by demonstrating fundamental differences in how layers 2/3 and 5 of the mouse primary visual cortex (V1) integrate sensory and behavioral information. The researchers found that layer 2/3 activity is predominantly modulated by visual input and is desynchronized by locomotion, whereas layer 5 activity is more strongly influenced by movement and exhibits increased synchrony during motor action. Furthermore, spontaneous activity in layer 2/3 is lower-dimensional and dominated by a single coding dimension compared to the higher-dimensional activity in layer 5. These findings suggest a functional division of labor where superficial layers specialize in robust sensory representations for downstream cortical processing, while deep layers integrate sensory signals with behavioral context to guide motor output.

### 2025 — Visual experience orthogonalizes visual cortical stimulus responses via population code transformation
*Cell Reports*
Authors: Samuel W. Failor, Matteo Carandini, Kenneth D. Harris

This study investigates how behavioral training alters stimulus representation in the mouse primary visual cortex (V1). By measuring orientation tuning in 4,000-neuron populations, the authors identified that training on a visuomotor task induces a specific mathematical transformation of population activity. This transformation selectively suppresses responses to motor-associated stimuli in neurons that otherwise fire at intermediate levels, resulting in the sparsening and orthogonalization of population codes. The degree of this transformation fluctuates trial-to-trial, suggesting it is driven by dynamic circuit mechanisms rather than static synaptic plasticity. While this orthogonalization does not enhance the performance of an optimal stimulus decoder, it significantly improves the accuracy of suboptimal decoders with inductive biases, suggesting that learning-induced plasticity in V1 facilitates the readout of task-relevant information by downstream neural circuits.

### 2024 — Visuomotor association orthogonalizes visual cortical population codes
*bioRxiv (Revised version)*
Authors: Samuel W. Failor, Matteo Carandini, Kenneth D. Harris

The authors measured orientation tuning in large populations of mouse V1 neurons before and after training on a visuomotor task to determine if task demands reshape cortical codes. They observed that training results in a population-level sparsening and orthogonalization of neural representations for motor-associated stimuli. This effect was captured by a simple mathematical transformation where responses to task-relevant stimuli were suppressed in cells with intermediate-level firing. The strength of this transformation was found to vary across trials, indicating it arises from dynamic circuit states rather than fixed synaptic changes. The results suggest that the primary visual cortex undergoes a representational shift that specifically differentiates task-relevant stimuli, thereby aiding downstream decision-making systems.
