# Ian Charest

*cognitive computational neuroscience of vision* — Université de Montréal, Département de psychologie, The Charest Lab.

## Background

Ian Charest is an associate professor and Courtois chairholder in cognitive and computational neuroscience who investigates how the human brain processes high-level visual and auditory information. Charest’s research utilizes a multi-methodological approach that combines functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and magneto-electroencephalography (M-EEG) with computational modeling, including representational similarity analysis (RSA) and artificial neural networks. A focus of their work involves characterizing individual representational idiosyncrasies and understanding the interaction between vision and semantics during the recognition of natural scenes.

## Papers

### 2025 — High-level visual representations in the human brain are aligned with large language models
*Nature Machine Intelligence*
Authors: Adrien Doerig, Tim C. Kietzmann, Emily Allen, Yihan Wu, Thomas Naselaris, Kendrick Kay, Ian Charest

This study investigates whether the complex visual information extracted by the human brain from natural scenes—including object interrelations and environmental context—is effectively modeled by the representational format of large language models (LLMs). Utilizing the 7T fMRI Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD), the authors demonstrate that LLM embeddings derived from scene captions successfully characterize neural activity across an extensive network of brain regions. Through controlled model comparisons, they show that the alignment between brain and model representations arises from the LLMs' capacity to integrate relational information beyond the sum of individual words. Furthermore, deep neural networks (DNNs) trained to map visual inputs into this caption-based semantic space exhibit greater brain-model similarity than traditional object-recognition models, suggesting that visuo-semantic transformation constitutes a fundamental objective of the human visual system.

### 2025 — Representations in vision and language converge in a shared, multidimensional space of perceived similarities
*bioRxiv (Preprint)*
Authors: Katerina Marie Simkova, Adrien Doerig, Clayton Hickey, Ian Charest

The research explores the hypothesis that human sensory systems transform inputs into a shared, modality-agnostic representational space where visual and linguistic information converge. Participants performed behavioral similarity judgments on natural scene images and their corresponding textual captions from the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD). Representational similarity analysis (RSA) revealed that both visual and linguistic similarity structures not only converged at the behavioral level but also accurately predicted fMRI responses in a consistent network of mid-to-high-level visual brain regions. Furthermore, computational models trained to align images with LLM-based embeddings outperformed standard category-trained networks in capturing this shared behavioral similarity structure. These findings suggest that conceptual representations are grounded in a commonRelational code that reflects the stable structure of the environment, transcending the specific sensory modality of input.

### 2024 — Neural computations in prosopagnosia
*Cerebral Cortex*
Authors: Simon Faghel-Soubeyrand, Anne-Raphaelle Richoz, Delphine Waeber, Jessica Woodhams, Roberto Caldara, Frédéric Gosselin, Ian Charest

This investigation identifies the computational deficits underlying the loss of face identification ability in brain-lesioned patient PS, a classic case of acquired pure prosopagnosia. By collecting a large-scale dataset of high-density electroencephalography (EEG) and applying representational similarity analysis (RSA), the authors compared time-resolved brain representations against various computational models, including deep convolutional neural networks (DNNs) and semantic embeddings. The study revealed that PS's neural representations exhibited excessive generalization of early visual patterns, remaining more similar to early layers of visual DNNs than those of neurotypical controls. Crucially, PS showed significant representational deficits in high-level visual and semantic computations, particularly during the N170 and P300 processing windows. These results provide mechanistic evidence that impaired face recognition is linked to a failure in constructing abstract, high-level representational geometries within the ventral visual stream.

### 2024 — Decoding face recognition abilities in the human brain
*PNAS Nexus*
Authors: Simon Faghel-Soubeyrand, Meike Ramon, Eva Bamps, Matteo Zoia, Jessica Woodhams, Anne-Raphaelle Richoz, Roberto Caldara, Frédéric Gosselin, Ian Charest

This study employs a data-driven approach to characterize the neuro-computational variations across the face recognition spectrum, ranging from typical recognizers to 'super-recognizers' (individuals with exceptional face identification skills). Using high-density EEG and multivariate pattern analysis (MVPA), the authors successfully decoded individual face recognition ability scores from just one second of brain activity with high accuracy. Comparisons with DNN models of vision and semantics indicated that super-recognizers exhibit enhanced mid-level visual computations at early latencies and richer semantic representations at later latencies (approx. 600–700 ms). These findings provide the first direct empirical evidence linking high-level semantic brain computations to individual differences in face recognition ability, suggesting that superior performance is supported by more robust integration of visual and conceptual features.
