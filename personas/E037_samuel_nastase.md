# Samuel Nastase

*computational cognitive neuroscience* — Princeton University, Department of Psychology, Shared Minds Lab.

## Background

Samuel Nastase investigates the neural mechanisms underlying human communication and shared cognition using naturalistic stimuli such as narratives and cinema. Nastase employs computational techniques, including hyperalignment and intersubject correlation, to map high-dimensional representational spaces shared across individual brains. Their recent work focuses on integrating large language models with neuroimaging data to understand how linguistic structures facilitate the transmission of information during social interaction.

## Papers

### 2025 — Aligning brains into a shared space improves their alignment with large language models
*Nature Computational Science*
Authors: Arnab Bhattacharjee, Zaid Zada, Haocheng Wang, Bobbi Aubrey, Werner Doyle, Patricia Dugan, Daniel Friedman, Orrin Devinsky, Adeen Flinker, Peter J Ramadge, Uri Hasson, Ariel Goldstein, Samuel A. Nastase

This study demonstrates that large language models (LLMs) can effectively predict word-by-word neural activity recorded via electrocorticography (ECoG) during naturalistic speech comprehension. To overcome the limitations of electrode-level and subject-specific encoding models, the researchers utilized a Shared Response Model (SRM) to estimate a common information space across a cohort of participants listening to a 30-minute podcast. Projections into this shared response space significantly enhanced the predictive accuracy of LLM-based encoding models and allowed for the denoising of individual brain signals, resulting in a 37% mean improvement in encoding accuracy. These gains were localized primarily to the superior temporal gyrus and inferior frontal gyrus, suggesting that shared responses across individuals provide a more robust target for modeling the neural representations of language.

### 2025 — Cortical language areas are coupled via a soft hierarchy of model-based linguistic features
*bioRxiv*
Authors: Ahmad Samara, Zaid Zada, Tamara Vanderwal, Uri Hasson, Samuel A. Nastase

This work posits that regions within the human language network coordinate through shared subspaces of linguistic features. The researchers developed a model-based functional connectivity framework using three classes of embeddings—acoustic, speech, and high-level language—extracted from a unified neural network model. By applying this framework to naturalistic fMRI data of narrative comprehension, they identified a soft hierarchy of stimulus-driven connectivity. Early auditory regions were coupled via low-level acoustic and speech features, whereas higher-order lateral temporal and default-mode regions were primarily coupled through abstract linguistic features. These results suggest that feature-specific communication channels facilitate the integration of complex information across the cortical language network.

### 2025 — Hierarchical systems in the default mode network when reasoning about self and other mental states
*bioRxiv*
Authors: Isaac R. Christian, Samuel A. Nastase

The study utilizes fMRI and multi-voxel pattern analysis (MVPA) to investigate how the default mode network (DMN) handles mental state inference for the 'self' compared to 'other' agents. Participants performed tasks requiring them to monitor their own internal states or the mental states of individuals depicted in videos. The results identified a functional hierarchy along the medial prefrontal cortex: ventral regions selectively decoded mental state patterns specific to the self, while dorsal regions and the posterior cingulate cortex represented general social reasoning processes applicable to both targets. These findings suggest that the social brain contains both specialized modules for self-reflection and general-purpose systems for modeling mental states.

### 2024 — Shared functional specialization in transformer-based language models and the human brain
*Nature Communications*
Authors: Sreejan Kumar, Theodore R. Sumers, Takateru Yamakoshi, Ariel Goldstein, Uri Hasson, Kenneth A. Norman, Thomas L. Griffiths, Robert D. Hawkins, Samuel A. Nastase

The research investigates whether the circuit computations in Transformer-based language models correspond to specialized functional processes in the human brain. Rather than analyzing static word embeddings, the authors deconstructed the model's internal operations into specific 'transformations'—the computations that integrate context across words. Using fMRI data from subjects listening to naturalistic narratives, they verified that these transformation-based circuits account for substantial variance in the cortical language network. Crucially, the study found that specific attention heads within the Transformer architecture differentially predict neural activity in distinct brain regions, revealing a correspondence between artificial circuit specialization and cortical gradients of layer depth and contextual integration.

### 2024 — Behaviorally-relevant features of observed actions dominate cortical representational geometry in natural vision
*bioRxiv*
Authors: Jane Han, Vassiki Chauhan, Rebecca Philip, Morgan K. Taylor, Heejung Jung, Yaroslav O. Halchenko, M. Ida Gobbini, James V. Haxby, Samuel A. Nastase

This fMRI study examines the neural representational geometry of action understanding using dynamic, real-world video stimuli. The researchers compared the predictive power of behavioral models (judgments of an action's purpose and sociality) against semantic models and low-level visual feature models. They found that behavioral models of transitivity (action purpose) and sociality (social content) significantly outperformed visual and semantic models in predicting neural representations across much of the cortex. These behaviorally relevant action features explained unique variance not only in the traditional action observation network but also in ventral temporal regions, indicating that high-level behavioral dimensions are central to how the brain organizes information about dynamic social interactions.
