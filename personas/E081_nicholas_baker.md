# Nicholas Baker

*computational vision and shape perception* — Loyola University Chicago, Department of Psychology, Human Perception Lab.

## Background

Nicholas Baker is an Assistant Professor of Neuroscience in the Department of Psychology at Loyola University Chicago and directs the Human Perception Lab. Baker’s research examines the information processing systems that transform local visual contours into abstract shape representations, building upon Gestalt principles. Using psychophysical methodologies and computational modeling, Baker investigates the nature of shape abstraction in human vision and compares these processes to the mechanisms of deep convolutional neural networks (DCNNs). Their work identifies discrepancies between biological vision and artificial intelligence, specifically regarding the sensitivity of deep networks to global object configuration.

## Papers

### 2025 — Three-dimensional shape cues affect human and artificial recognition systems differently
*bioRxiv*
Authors: Mikayla Cutler, Luke Baumel, Joseph Tocco, William Friebel, George K. Thiruvathukal, Nicholas Baker

This research investigates the divergent utilization of shape and texture information by human observers and deep neural networks (DNNs), specifically focusing on 3D volumetric cues. While human object recognition is primarily driven by global shape, DNNs exhibit a pronounced bias toward local texture. Prior evaluations of this bias have largely utilized 2D external contours; however, 3D structure is also conveyed via internal contours, shading, and attached shadows, particularly under noncanonical viewing conditions. The authors developed the ReTexture dataset, comprising 120,000 images where objects from ShapeNet were rendered with texture substitution across multiple viewpoints, both with and without 3D shading cues. Behavioral and computational experiments demonstrated that human performance in classifying texture-substituted objects by shape significantly exceeded that of all tested DNNs. Interestingly, the inclusion of 3D cues reduced the texture bias in neural networks. Furthermore, while 3D cues primarily enhanced human recognition for objects in noncanonical orientations, they provided the greatest performance gains for DNNs in canonical views. The findings suggest a fundamental mechanistic distinction: humans utilize shading and shadows to infer 3D volumetric structure, whereas artificial systems treat these cues as additional surface-level features analogous to texture.
