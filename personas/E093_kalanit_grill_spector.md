# Kalanit Grill-Spector

*Visual perception and neurodevelopment* — Stanford University, Department of Psychology, Vision and Perception Neuroscience Lab.

## Background

Kalanit Grill-Spector is a professor of psychology whose research examines the neural mechanisms of visual perception and their maturation throughout the human lifespan. Grill-Spector developed the fMRI-adaptation technique to probe the functional properties of cortical neurons and conducted studies on the ventral temporal cortex's role in face and object recognition. Their laboratory employs multimodal neuroimaging and computational modeling, including deep neural networks, to investigate how structural connectivity and visual experience drive the development of specialized brain regions. This research focuses on the relationship between cortical organization and the behavioral improvement of recognition abilities from infancy to adulthood.

## Papers

### 2025 — A single computational objective can produce specialization of streams in visual cortex
*bioRxiv*
Authors: Dawn Finzi, Eshed Margalit, Kendrick Kay, Daniel L. K. Yamins, Kalanit Grill-Spector

The authors evaluate the computational origins of the segregation of human visual cortex into ventral, lateral, and dorsal processing streams. Utilizing the large-scale Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD) and Topographic Deep Artificial Neural Networks (TDANNs), they test whether task-specific behavioral demands (e.g., action vs. object recognition) or a unified architectural constraint drives this functional differentiation. They find that a self-supervised model optimized for representational learning while minimizing local spatial wiring costs—implemented via a correlation-based topographic loss—replicates the large-scale topography of the visual streams better than task-specialized models. The TDANN successfully predicts voxel-wise neural responses and the spatial organization of category-selective clusters and motion sensitivity across streams, suggesting that functional organization arises from a single principle of balancing general representation learning with local spatial constraints.

### 2025 — How infant brains fold: Sulcal deepening is linked to development of sulcal span, thickness, curvature, and microstructure
*bioRxiv · 2 citations*
Authors: Sarah S. Tung, Xiaoqian Yan, Bella Fascendini, Christina Tyagi, Charleny Martinez Reyes, Keithan Ducre, Karla Perez, Ahmad Allen, Juliet Horenziak, Hua Wu, Boris Keil, Vaidehi S. Natu, Kalanit Grill-Spector

Cortical folding continues postnatally as sulci deepen, yet the timeline and underlying mechanisms of this maturation are poorly understood. Through longitudinal structural and quantitative magnetic resonance imaging (qMRI) in infants from birth to one year of age, the researchers measured macroanatomical and microstructural maturation in primary and secondary sulci. They found that sulci emerging earlier in utero are deeper at birth and deepen at a slower rate postnatally than later-emerging sulci. Analysis of local depth reveals that deeper portions of sulci (fundi) exhibit higher curvature and higher microstructural density (R1 relaxation rate) compared to surrounding walls. Mean sulcal depth was found to be predicted by a weighted sum of sulcal span, thickness, curvature, and tissue microstructure, indicating that postnatal brain folding is a multifactorial process rather than being driven by a single mechanical force.

### 2025 — White matter connections of human ventral temporal cortex are organized by cytoarchitecture, eccentricity, and category-selectivity from birth
*Nature Human Behaviour · 10 citations*
Authors: Emily Kubota, Xiaoqian Yan, Sarah Tung, Bella Fascendini, Christina Tyagi, Sophie Duhameau, Danya Ortiz, Mareike Grotheer, Vaidehi S. Natu, Boris Keil, Kalanit Grill-Spector

This study investigates whether the functional organization of the human ventral temporal cortex (VTC) is scaffolded by innate structural connectivity. Using custom baby-specific MRI coils and high-resolution diffusion imaging in neonates and infants (0-6 months), the authors mapped the probabilistic white matter connectivity profiles of the VTC. They found that connectivity is organized by cytoarchitecture, eccentricity biases, and category-selectivity from birth. Connectivity profiles of functional regions within the same cytoarchitectonic area are similar at birth and develop in parallel, characterized by increasing connectivity to the lateral prefrontal cortex and decreasing connectivity to lateral occipital and parietal regions. These results suggest that innate white matter connections provide a stable structural blueprint that precedes and likely constrains the development of functional category-selectivity in the human brain.

### 2024 — The emergence of visual category representations in infants' brains
*eLife · 9 citations*
Authors: Xiaoqian Yan, Sarah Shi Tung, Bella Fascendini, Yulan Diana Chen, Anthony M Norcia, Kalanit Grill-Spector

The researchers characterized the development of neural representations for faces, limbs, corridors, characters, and cars using steady-state visual evoked potential (SSVEP) electroencephalography in infants aged 3 to 15 months. While robust visual responses over early visual cortex were present at 3-4 months, category-selective representations emerged hierarchically: face-selective responses were reliable by 4-6 months, while responses to limbs and corridors emerged around 6-8 months. Spatiotemporal multivariate pattern analysis (MVPA) indicated that category distinctiveness increases significantly between 6 and 15 months, allowing for robust decoding of visual categories from neural signals. These findings suggest that category representations are learned at different rates during infancy, with categories of high ecological significance showing earlier neural specialization.

### 2024 — Rethinking simultaneous suppression in visual cortex via compressive spatiotemporal population receptive fields
*Nature Communications · 5 citations*
Authors: Eline R. Kupers, Insub Kim, Kalanit Grill-Spector

Simultaneous suppression, where neural responses to concurrent stimuli are lower than the sum of responses to individual stimuli, is often attributed to resource competition or attention. The authors propose a stimulus-driven computational account based on a compressive spatiotemporal population receptive field (st-pRF) model. Using fMRI, they show that suppression occurs in single voxels, varies with stimulus size and timing, and increases across the visual hierarchy. The st-pRF model, which integrates spatial and temporal information through compressive nonlinearities, successfully predicts simultaneous suppression across the visual system without requiring task-dependent competition. This suggests that suppression is a fundamental outcome of the spatiotemporal summation properties of receptive fields, with larger pRF sizes and stronger nonlinearities in higher-order areas driving greater suppressive effects.
